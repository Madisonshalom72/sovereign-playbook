What the repository sale list actually is

Start with the ladder every tax-delinquent property in the county climbs. When a property owner falls behind on taxes, the claim moves to the Luzerne County Tax Claim Bureau (the Bureau's operations run through Elite Revenue Solutions, at luzernecountytaxclaim.com). If the taxes stay unpaid, the property is exposed at the upset sale, where the opening price is set by what is owed. Properties that sell move on. Properties that draw no bid at all drop onto the Repository Sale List — the county's standing inventory of unsold tax-delinquent land and buildings.

That list is a living document. The Bureau republishes it as parcels are added and sold off; the October 1, 2026 edition runs to 691 parcels across the county — city lots in Wilkes-Barre, rural acreage in the townships, houses, rear lots, even subsurface-only parcels. Some entries already carry a "Bid Pending" flag, meaning someone else's written bid is working its way through the process described below. The rest are open to a bid from anyone who follows the process and survives it.

The first surprise: there is no separate private sale list

Most people go looking for two documents — a repository list and a private sale list — and waste time hunting for the second one. It does not exist. The Bureau publishes no separate private-sale property list. "Private sale" is not an inventory; it is the process — the written-bid procedure for buying a property that already went unsold at the upset sale. The Repository Sale List is the inventory for that process. When you hear "tax claim bureau private sale," translate it in your head to: pick a parcel off the repository list, then run the bid gauntlet below.

What your bid must cover — the full stack of costs

Here is the number that surprises first-time bidders: you do not get to name a lowball price and wait. A repository bid must cover the full amount owed against the parcel, plus the Bureau's process costs:

Read the list honestly

The dollar figure printed beside each parcel on the repository list is its assessed value. Assessed value is not the bid price — your bid is set by the taxes, interest, and penalties owed, which the list does not print — and it is not market value either. A parcel assessed at $60,000 can carry a small tax bill or a crushing one. The only way to know your real floor on a specific parcel is to ask the Bureau for the amount owed on that parcel.

The private sale process, step by step

  1. Pick your parcel off the repository sale list. Work from the current edition — the list changes as parcels sell and new ones land. Note the parcel's PIN (parcel identification number); everything downstream keys off it.
  2. Get the amount owed. Ask the Tax Claim Bureau what stands against the parcel: delinquent taxes, interest, penalties, and current-year taxes. That total — not the assessed value — is the floor your bid must cover, before the $500 + $25 + $20 in process costs.
  3. Do your lien homework before you bid. Repository property is sold subject to existing liens (see the risks section below). Search the title and the lien records on the parcel first. Money spent on a search is cheap next to inheriting somebody else's judgment lien.
  4. Submit the written bid with escrow money. The bid goes to the Bureau in writing, and your bid money is placed in escrow as a certified or cashier's check — personal checks do not run this process.
  5. Sign the notarized affidavit. The Bureau requires a notarized affidavit with the bid. Budget the notary trip; an unsworn packet is an incomplete packet.
  6. Your bid gets advertised — twice. The Bureau advertises the bid two times. This is the step nobody warns you about: the advertising is public notice that a bid is on the table, and it opens the door in the next step.
  7. A competing bid turns your private sale into an auction. If another bidder answers the advertising with a higher bid, the "private" sale stops being private — the parcel goes to an auction between the competing bidders. The parcel you researched for weeks can be won by whoever shows up with more money at the end.
  8. All three taxing districts must approve. County, municipality, and school district each get a say. A sale is not final until all three taxing districts approve it — any one of them can stall or stop the deal.
  9. Wait. From bid to deed, the private sale process typically runs six months to one year. Escrow, advertising, competing-bid windows, and three separate approvals take the time they take. Plan your money and your patience accordingly.

The risks, stated plainly: liens, no warranty, and time

Sold subject to existing liens — with NO warranty

This is the sentence that decides whether repository buying makes you money or buries you. The Bureau sells repository property subject to existing liens and gives no warranty — not about title, not about the property's condition, not about what is owed against it beyond the taxes in your bid stack. A mortgage, judgment, or municipal lien riding on the parcel can survive your purchase and become your problem the day the deed records. The tax debt is the price of admission; it is not a promise that the title is clean. Search first, bid second — and if a parcel's lien picture is murky, the disciplined move is to walk away and pick another of the 691.

  • Your bid is a floor, not a price. The advertised bid can draw a competitor and become an auction. Never fall in love with a parcel at step one; decide your ceiling before the advertising runs.
  • Three approvals means three chances to die. County, municipality, and school district must all approve. A parcel can clear the money and still fail the politics.
  • Your capital sleeps for 6 to 12 months. Escrow goes in early; the deed comes late. Repository buying is patient money only.
  • Assessed value lies in both directions. It is neither what you will pay nor what the property is worth. It is a tax figure. Treat it as a sorting tool for the list — nothing more.

Repository sale FAQ

What is the Luzerne County repository sale?

When a tax-delinquent property is offered at the county upset sale and nobody bids on it, the property goes onto the Repository Sale List held by the Luzerne County Tax Claim Bureau. Those repository properties can then be purchased through the Bureau's private sale process — a written bid, not a live auction. The October 1, 2026 edition of the list holds 691 parcels.

Is there a separate private sale list?

No. The Tax Claim Bureau does not publish a separate private-sale property list. Private sale is the written-bid process for buying properties that went unsold at the upset sale — the standing inventory is the Repository Sale List itself.

How much do I have to bid on a repository property?

Your bid must cover all delinquent taxes, plus interest and penalties, plus the current year's taxes. On top of that the Bureau adds $500 for advertising and postage, $25 for deed preparation, and a $20 UPI fee. The assessed value printed on the list is not the bid price and is not market value — the amount owed on the parcel is what sets the floor.

Can someone outbid me on a private sale?

Yes. Your bid is advertised twice, and if a competing bid comes in during that advertising, the property goes to an auction between the bidders. All three taxing districts — county, municipality, and school district — must also approve the sale.

Do repository properties come with clear title?

No. Repository properties are sold subject to existing liens and with no warranty from the Bureau. Whatever liens ride with the property can survive the sale, which is why a title search and lien check before bidding are essential. The process also takes time — typically six months to a year from bid to deed.

Bottom line

The Luzerne County repository sale is not a secret and it is not a shortcut. It is 691 parcels nobody bid on, a written-bid process that charges you every dollar of tax owed plus $545 in process costs, an advertising window that can turn your deal into an auction, three taxing districts that all have to say yes, and a deed that arrives six months to a year later — with whatever liens were already on the land still attached. For the buyer who reads the list honestly and searches the title first, it is one of the few genuinely open doors left in real property. For everyone else, it is a very educational way to buy somebody else's liens.
