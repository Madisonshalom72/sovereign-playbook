Private Express Trust: How It Works, Step by Step

By Madison Hoskins • UCC Talk24 • Published November 16, 2026 • Educational guide — not legal advice

Motion graphic — animated, never a static picture.

A private express trust is one of the oldest tools in private law, and one of the most oversold. At its heart it is simple: one person, the grantor, gives property to another person, the trustee, to hold and manage for a third person, the beneficiary, on terms written down in a document called an indenture or declaration of trust. That is all it is, and that is also all it needs to be. It is a structure for holding and managing property according to the grantor's instructions. This guide explains what a private express trust actually is, the three roles in it, the steps to create one and fund it, and the honest limits — the things a trust cannot do, no matter what a sales pitch promises.

What a private express trust is

A private express trust is a trust created expressly by the grantor's declaration or indenture — in plain words, by the grantor saying so, in writing, and meaning it. It is not created by a court, and it is not imposed by operation of law, the way a constructive trust or a resulting trust can be. It is a private arrangement, made by private people, for private purposes. The word “express” is the key: the grantor's intent to create a trust must be expressed, clearly, in the document. A vague wish, a note to self, or a verbal understanding is not enough to create a trust that a bank, a court, or a buyer will recognise. The document should name the trust, name the trustee, name the beneficiaries, describe the property being placed in trust, state the trustee's duties and powers, state how and when the beneficiaries receive income or principal, and state what happens when the trust ends. It should be signed by the grantor, and the trustee should accept it in writing. That is the whole foundation. Everything else — the tax identification number, the bank account, the property titles — is built on top of that foundation, and none of it works without it. A trust is not a person, even though it can hold property, open accounts, and be taxed. It is a relationship, and the relationship is defined by the document that creates it. Write that document carefully, and have it reviewed by a licensed attorney in your state, because trust law varies by state and a poorly drafted indenture can fail at the moment it matters most.

The three roles

Every trust has three roles, and keeping them straight is the difference between a trust that works and one that collapses into confusion. The grantor — also called the settlor, the trustor, or the creator — is the person who creates the trust and transfers property into it. The grantor writes the rules: who the trustee is, who the beneficiaries are, what the trustee may and may not do, and when the trust ends. In a revocable trust, the grantor may keep the right to change the terms or take the property back. In an irrevocable trust, the grantor generally gives that power up, and the transfer is much harder to undo. Choose the type with your eyes open, and with advice, because that choice has tax, control, and creditor consequences that last for years. The trustee is the person or institution that holds legal title to the trust property and manages it for the beneficiaries. The trustee's job is not to own the property for themselves, but to hold it, protect it, invest it prudently, keep records, file the trust's tax returns, and distribute income and principal exactly as the indenture requires. A trustee owes the beneficiaries fiduciary duties — loyalty, care, and good faith — and a trustee who uses trust property for personal benefit, or who ignores the indenture, can be removed and held liable. The beneficiary is the person the trust is for. Beneficiaries hold the equitable interest: they are entitled to the benefits of the property, on the terms the grantor set, even though the trustee holds the legal title. The same person can hold more than one role — a grantor can also be a trustee, or a beneficiary — but the roles must not be blurred in a way that defeats the trust. If the grantor is also the sole trustee and the sole beneficiary, and can take everything back at any time, there may be little left that is truly “in trust.” Keep the roles honest, and keep them documented.

Step by step: creating the trust

  1. Decide the purpose. Write down, in one paragraph, what the trust is for: holding a family home, managing money for a child, holding a business, or keeping property organised for the next generation. The purpose shapes every choice that follows.
  2. Draft the indenture. Put the trust in writing. Name the trust, the grantor, the trustee, and the beneficiaries. Describe the property, the trustee's powers and duties, the distribution rules, the successor trustee, and how the trust ends. Have a licensed attorney review it before anyone signs.
  3. Name the trustee — and a successor. Choose someone competent, honest, and willing to do the work, and name a successor trustee in case the first cannot serve. A trustee who will not act is as bad as no trustee at all.
  4. Create and accept the trust. The grantor signs the indenture, and the trustee accepts it in writing. Some states require the document to be notarised; check your state's rules and follow them.
  5. Get an EIN if needed. If the trust will file its own tax return, or open accounts in its own name, it will usually need an Employer Identification Number from the IRS. A revocable grantor trust that reports under the grantor's Social Security number may not need a separate EIN while the grantor is living; an irrevocable trust generally will.
  6. Open accounts in the trust's name. Take the signed indenture and the EIN, if you have one, to the bank, and open accounts titled in the name of the trust — not in the grantor's personal name. The account title is what makes the account a trust account.

The step people skip: funding it

Funding is the step that turns a signed document into a working trust, and it is the step most often skipped. A trust is funded when assets are actually transferred into it — retitled into the trust's name. An unfunded trust protects nothing. It holds nothing, it manages nothing, and it distributes nothing, because there is nothing in it. To fund a trust, change the title on each asset: record a new deed for real property, transferring it to the trustee of the trust; change the title on vehicles; re-register investment and bank accounts in the trust's name; and assign business interests, notes, and other property to the trust. Keep a schedule of the assets the trust holds, and update it every time an asset is added or removed. Do not leave a house, a car, or an account in your personal name and assume the trust covers it because the indenture mentions it. It does not. The law looks at who holds title, and a title in your personal name is your personal property, however good the trust document is. Funding takes time, paperwork, and follow-up with banks, title companies, and county recorders. Do it, and keep proof of each transfer. A trust that is created but never funded is the most common, and most avoidable, failure in this whole subject.

Honest limits

A trust is a structure, not a magic shield. Say it plainly, because the sales pitches never do: a trust does not erase lawful debts, taxes, or court orders. If you owe money, putting property in a trust does not make the debt disappear. If you owe taxes, a trust does not make the tax go away — and in many cases the trust itself is taxed, sometimes at higher rates than an individual. If a court orders you to pay, or to turn over property, a trust does not put you beyond the court's reach. A transfer made to defraud creditors — a fraudulent transfer — can be undone, and courts can reach the property, hold the transferor liable, and, in the worst cases, impose penalties. That is true whether the trust is revocable or irrevocable, and whether the transfer was made last week or last year. A trust also does not make you immune to the law: a trustee who breaks the law in the trust's name is still liable, and a beneficiary who receives trust income still reports it. What a trust can do is real, and it is enough: it can hold property according to your instructions, keep it managed when you cannot manage it yourself, provide for beneficiaries on your terms, avoid probate for property that is properly funded into it, and keep private arrangements private. Those are valuable uses, and they are the reason trusts have been used for centuries. They are also the honest uses. Do not buy a trust because someone promised it would make you untouchable. Buy the work of a good attorney, fund the trust properly, and use it for what it is.

Common mistakes

Most trust failures are not failures of law, but failures of follow-through. The indenture is copied from the internet and never reviewed, so it names the wrong state's law, leaves out a successor trustee, or contradicts itself. The trustee is chosen for loyalty alone, with no regard for competence, and the trust is never administered. The EIN is never obtained, so no account can be opened in the trust's name. The accounts are opened in the grantor's name anyway. The deed is never recorded, so the house is still personally owned. The trust is never funded, so it holds nothing. The grantor keeps treating trust property as personal property, spending it, selling it, or pledging it without regard to the indenture — and then wonders why a court treats the trust as a sham. Or the trust is created in a rush, after a lawsuit has already been filed or a debt has already come due, in an attempt to move assets out of reach. That is the worst mistake of all, because it is the one the law is designed to undo, and it can turn a civil debt into a fraud claim. The fix is unglamorous: get advice before you create the trust, fund it properly, keep records, respect the roles, and use the trust for the purpose you wrote down at the start.
