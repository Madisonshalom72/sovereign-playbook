How to Protect Your Assets From a Judgment

By Madison Hoskins • UCC Talk24 • Published November 13, 2026 • Educational guide — not legal advice

If you want to learn how to protect your assets from a judgment, start with the public record of a man who didn't — because everything he built sat in his own name. Damon Dash co-founded Roc-A-Fella Records. In November 2024, the United States Marshals auctioned his one-third of the label to satisfy a judgment, and the winning bidder was the State of New York, collecting on millions in back taxes. His film company followed, then a bankruptcy petition listing $25.3 million in debts against $4,350 in assets, then — in October 2026 — the sale of the rights to his own life story. This page walks the whole record, then teaches the structure that changes the ending: what a private trust honestly does about a judgment, where its protection ends, and the five moves that decide which story yours becomes. The other half of the lesson — a fortune that survived because the paperwork was signed early — is the Michael Jackson estate story.

What happened to Damon Dash: the auction record

Damon Dash co-founded Roc-A-Fella Records with Jay-Z and Kareem "Biggs" Burke. The piece that outlived the partnership was his one-third interest in Roc-A-Fella Inc., the company holding the rights to Jay-Z's debut album, Reasonable Doubt. That interest sat where most people keep everything they own: in his personal name.

So when the judgments came, they came for him personally — and everything he personally owned was reachable. In February 2024, a federal magistrate judge in New York authorized the U.S. Marshals Service to seize and auction his Roc-A-Fella interest to satisfy an $823,284 judgment owed to filmmaker Josh Webber and Muddy Water Pictures from the Dear Frank film litigation. The sale was delayed while bigger claims lined up behind it: more than $193,000 in unpaid child support and over $8.7 million in New York back taxes, with an agreed payment order that put child support first.

On November 14, 2024, the auction ran. New York State won his one-third of Roc-A-Fella with a $1 million credit bid — the state didn't even need cash, because Dash owed it far more than the bid. A founder's stake in one of the most important labels in hip-hop history changed hands without a single dollar moving, to settle a personal tax bill.

It did not stop there. On September 4, 2025, Dash filed Chapter 7 bankruptcy in Florida. His petition — reported by PEOPLE from the court documents — listed $25,303,049.47 in liabilities owed to roughly 49 creditors, against $4,350 in assets. About $19.1 million of the debt was taxes and other government obligations; his domestic support obligations exceeded $647,000; his listed monthly income was $0. In December 2025, the Marshals auctioned his film company, Poppington LLC — it sold for $100.50. And in October 2026, the bankruptcy trustee auctioned the commercial rights to his life story itself — his name, voice, image and likeness — with Everlasting Films winning at $550,000, over his objection.

Why it was all reachable: his name was on everything

The pattern

Notice what the courts actually took: not "his business" in the abstract — his personal property. Personally held shares. Personally owed taxes. Personally signed obligations. When everything you build is titled to you, every creditor you ever have is standing in the same line, and the court holds the door.

A judgment is enforced against property of the judgment debtor. That phrase is the whole story. The Marshals did not need a new theory for each asset — the shares, the company, the likeness rights — because each one answered to the same name. One balance sheet, one target. Dash's problem was never a lack of assets across his career; it was that at the moment of enforcement, the assets and the debts carried the identical owner: him, personally.

That is the default setting for most builders, contractors, landlords and creators: the business interest in your name, the property in your name, the accounts in your name. It feels simple because it is simple — right up until a judgment turns that simplicity into a single point of failure.

What a private express trust actually is — in Black's words

A private express trust is a trust created deliberately, by a written instrument, for private beneficiaries — a family, a business succession, an estate — as opposed to a charitable trust or a trust a court imposes. Here is the definition this lane works from, quoted verbatim from Madison's own copy of Black's Law Dictionary, 4th Edition Revised, entry TRUST, printed page 1680:

Black's Law Dictionary, 4th Ed. Revised — TRUST, p. 1680

"A right of property, real or personal, held by one party for the benefit of another. … A confidence reposed in one person, who is termed trustee, for the benefit of another, who is called the cestui que trust, respecting property which is held by the trustee for the benefit of the cestui que trust."

And Black's gives the essential elements in one sentence — a designated beneficiary and trustee, a "fund sufficiently identified to enable title to pass to trustee, and actual delivery to trustee with intention of passing title." Read that twice. A trust is not a magic word or a status you declare. It is a transfer: identified property, actually delivered, to a named trustee, who holds legal title under a duty to use it for the beneficiaries. How structure answers a judgment — and how it doesn't

The mechanism fits in one distinction:

  • Owning means the asset is yours — titled to you, listed on your balance sheet, reachable by your creditors, distributed by a probate court when you die, and inventoried by a trustee if you land in bankruptcy. This was Dash's column, top to bottom.
  • Holding means a trustee holds legal title to the asset under the trust's written terms, for the beneficiaries. A judgment against you personally is a claim against your property; property genuinely transferred to a trust, before trouble, in a real transaction, is a different legal pile — administered privately, passed by the trust's terms, and kept operating through disputes because the trust, not your personal balance sheet, is the constant.

What structure changes, stated plainly: who holds title, and when they came to hold it. What it never changes: whether the debt exists. The honest limits — read this before you believe anyone's pitch

This is the section the gurus skip. A trust is a real legal structure with real effects — and hard edges. Anyone who sells you past these edges is running the same kind of pitch exposed in our UCC-1 filing scams investigation.

Limit 1: Timing is the whole ballgame — structure is built before trouble

Transfers made when debts already exist — or made to hinder, delay, or defraud creditors — can be unwound by courts under fraudulent-transfer law (state UFTA/UVTA statutes and the Bankruptcy Code's lookback provisions). Moving your house into a trust after the judgment lands is not protection; it is evidence. Dash in 2024 could not have fixed Dash in 2024. The Jackson plan, in the companion story, worked because it was signed in 2002 — seven years before it was needed.

Limit 2: A trust does not erase taxes, judgments, or child support

No structure on this page makes a tax bill, a court judgment, or a support obligation disappear. Remember the order of payment at the Dash auction: child support stood first in line. Government claims and support obligations sit at the top of every priority system in American law, and a trustee who promises you otherwise is selling fiction. What legitimate structure changes is who holds title and how assets are administered — it is not a delete key for debts you lawfully owe.

Limit 3: The common revocable living trust is not a creditor shield

The standard revocable living trust — the one most estate planners sell for probate avoidance — generally does not protect assets from the grantor's own creditors, because you kept the power to take everything back. Probate avoidance and privacy are real benefits; whether a structure can honestly be called an asset protection trust is a different, narrower question that turns on the type of structure, your state's law, the timing, and the facts. That analysis is attorney work, and this page's disclaimer below means it.

Five moves: the structure, in order

  1. Inventory your personal name. List everything titled to you personally — business shares, real estate, accounts, vehicles, intellectual property, royalties. You cannot structure what you have not listed. (Dash's list, in the end, was made by a bankruptcy trustee. Make yours yourself.)
  2. Form the trust while the water is calm. A written declaration of trust: grantor, trustee, beneficiaries, terms, in a season when no creditor is knocking. Early and clean is the entire legal difference between planning and fraudulent transfer.
  3. Fund it — actually transfer title. Retitle the assets into the trustee's hands per Black's elements: identified property, delivered, with the intention of passing title. Deeds, assignments, and account titles are the trust, in operational terms. Skipping this step is its own famous failure — see the Jackson funding fight.
  4. Choose trustees like your legacy depends on it. It does. A trustee holds title and runs the terms when you can't. Pick people (or institutions) with the competence and the character, and name successors.
  5. Keep it real, separate, and documented. No commingling trust assets with personal spending, records kept, taxes filed correctly, terms followed. Courts respect structures that were run like they meant something. A structure treated as a costume gets treated as one.

Questions, answered straight

How do you protect your assets from a judgment?

Before there is a judgment — not after. Assets genuinely transferred, in a calm season, to a properly formed private trust are held by the trustee under the trust's written terms, for the beneficiaries; a judgment against you personally reaches your property, and the trust's property is a different legal pile. Transfers made after debts exist, or to hinder or defraud creditors, can be unwound as fraudulent transfers. Timing and honest paperwork are the protection; there is no filing that erases a judgment.

What happened to Damon Dash's Roc-A-Fella shares?

A federal court authorized the U.S. Marshals to auction his one-third interest in Roc-A-Fella Inc. to satisfy an $823,284 film-litigation judgment. With over $8.7 million in New York back taxes and child-support arrears also in line, New York State won the November 14, 2024 auction with a $1 million credit bid. Dash later filed Chapter 7 bankruptcy (September 4, 2025) listing $25.3 million in liabilities against $4,350 in assets.

Can I move my assets into a trust if I already owe money or have a judgment?

That is exactly the transfer the law is built to undo. Transfers made to hinder, delay, or defraud existing creditors can be reversed as fraudulent transfers, and they can create personal liability on top of the original debt. Structure is a before-trouble discipline. If trouble is already here, the honest next step is a licensed attorney or a legitimate debt workout — not a filing package.

Does a trust protect assets from taxes or child support?

No. Taxes and child support are priority obligations that no private trust erases — at the Dash auction, child support stood first in the payment line, ahead of the state tax claim and the judgment creditor. Any promoter who tells you a trust makes those obligations vanish is running a scam, full stop.
