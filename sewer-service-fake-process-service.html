Sewer Service: The Fake Service That Produces Real Default Judgments

By Madison Hoskins • UCC Talk24 • Published November 16, 2026 • Educational guide — not legal advice

There is a name for the dirtiest trick in the debt-collection machine: sewer service. A process server is hired to hand you a summons and complaint — the papers that tell you that you are being sued and start your clock to answer. Instead, the papers are thrown away, and the server swears an affidavit saying you were served. You never hear a word. Your time to answer runs out. And a court enters a default judgment against you in a case you never knew existed. This page explains how sewer service works, why the law says the judgment it produces is void, how to read the affidavit for the lie, and the step-by-step fight to vacate it.

What sewer service is

Every lawsuit begins the same way: someone files a complaint, and then the defendant has to be told. That telling is called service of process, and in most cases it is done by a process server — a person hired to physically deliver the summons and complaint, then file a sworn affidavit of service with the court describing when, where, and on whom the papers were served. The court does not watch the delivery. The court reads the affidavit. That piece of paper is the court's entire proof that you were notified.

Sewer service is what happens when the server skips the delivery and keeps the affidavit. The papers go, in the trade's own ugly phrase, down the sewer — discarded, never delivered, sometimes never even printed — and the server signs and swears the affidavit of service anyway. From the court's side of the counter, the file looks perfect: complaint filed, affidavit of service on record, answer deadline passed, nobody appeared. The clerk and the judge see a defendant who was told and chose to ignore it. So the court does what the rules tell it to do with a defendant who does not answer: it enters a default judgment — the plaintiff wins everything it asked for, without proving a single fact.

Understand who profits. A high-volume sewer service process server is not paid for effort; he is paid per completed service, and a debt-collection firm may hand him hundreds of files at a time. Real service is slow — people are not home, addresses are stale, doors go unanswered. Fake service is instant. Every discarded summons is a "completed" delivery at full fee, and the false affidavit of service converts the fraud into court-proof paper. The lie is not a byproduct of the scheme. The lie is the product.

The one-sentence version

Sewer service is a judgment manufactured out of a sworn lie about notice — and notice is the one thing the Constitution does not let a court fake.

Why it works: jurisdiction begins with notice

To see why this fraud matters — and why it can be undone — you have to see what service of process actually is in the law. It is not a courtesy and it is not paperwork. Black's Law Dictionary defines process by its function: the writ or summons issued in a judicial proceeding to acquire jurisdiction of a person or his property. Process exists to acquire jurisdiction — that is its job description. And service of process (Black's, printed p. 1534) is the act that completes it: delivering or leaving the writ with the party to whom it ought to be delivered — usually a copy is served and the original shown.

The American cornerstone is Pennoyer v. Neff, 95 U.S. 714 (1878). A judgment had been entered against Neff, an Oregon landowner, in a case where he was never personally served and never appeared — and his land was later sold on that judgment. The Supreme Court voided the whole chain. Out of Pennoyer come the two doors through which a court gets power over a person: valid service, or voluntary appearance. Through neither door, no personal jurisdiction — and a judgment entered anyway is not merely appealable. It is void: a nullity the court never had power to enter.

The modern measure of notice is Mullane v. Central Hanover Bank, 339 U.S. 306 (1950): notice must be "reasonably calculated, under all the circumstances, to apprise interested parties of the pendency of the action and afford them an opportunity to present their objections." Read that standard against sewer service and the fraud is naked. A discarded summons is not notice "reasonably calculated" to reach anyone. It is the opposite of the thing the Fourteenth Amendment requires — a deprivation of property with no notice and no hearing at all. That is why the judgment it produces can be attacked at its root, even years later: the event that gives a court power over you — valid service — never happened.

How the victim finds out

Almost nobody discovers sewer service by learning they were sued. They discover it at the moment the judgment starts collecting:

  • A frozen bank account. The judgment creditor serves a restraining notice or levy, and your debit card declines at the register. This is the most common discovery — the New York Attorney General's office, in its sewer-service cases, noted that many victims "first learned they were being sued when they found their bank accounts frozen or their wages garnished."
  • Wage garnishment. Your paycheck arrives short, and payroll hands you an income execution for a creditor you may never have heard of.
  • A judgment on your record. A mortgage application, a refinance, a car loan, or a landlord's background check surfaces a judgment in a court you have never entered, in a case with a docket number you have never seen.
  • A knock that comes too late. A marshal or sheriff appears to execute on the judgment — the first piece of paper in the entire case that actually reaches you is the one that takes your property.

Notice the sequence. In an honest case, the first paper is the summons and the last is the judgment. In a sewer-service case the order is reversed: the judgment is the first notice you ever get. Everything in the fight below flows from that single fact — the case was decided before you knew it existed, on the strength of an affidavit that says otherwise.

Red flags in an affidavit of service

The affidavit of service is the document the whole judgment stands on, and it is the first document to get and read. Fraudulent affidavits are usually mass-produced, and mass production leaves fingerprints. Look for:

  • The wrong address. Service claimed at an address where you never lived, or had moved away from long before — sometimes an address from a years-old credit application.
  • The wrong person. The affidavit describes the person served — age, height, weight, hair, sex — and the description does not match you or anyone in your household. Boilerplate descriptions ("male, 5'9", 180 lbs") get recycled across hundreds of files.
  • Impossible times. The server claims to have served you at a time you can prove you were somewhere else: at work on a time clock, in a hospital, in another state, in custody. Keep the proof — it is the spine of your motion.
  • The impossible route. One server "serving" dozens of people in the same hour at addresses miles apart. When investigators pull a sewer service process server's whole book, the map itself becomes the confession.
  • Details that don't fit the place. A description of a door, a doorman, a relative, or a neighbor that does not exist at the real address — because the server was never there.
  • A volume signature. The same server's name on an industrial number of affidavits. One New York company, taken down in the enforcement cases below, claimed to have served 98,000 summonses and complaints in under two years. Nobody serves 98,000 people. Somebody signs 98,000 affidavits.

One caution as you read: a detail being wrong does not by itself prove fraud — honest servers make honest errors, and minor errors in service papers are sometimes excused. What you are looking for is the pattern that shows the delivery never happened at all, backed by your own sworn statement and whatever records prove where you actually were.

The fight, step by step: vacate the judgment

The strategy in one line: you are not asking the court for mercy, and you are not re-arguing the debt. You are showing the court that the event which gives it power over you — valid service — never occurred, so the judgment is void and must be vacated. Here is the sequence:

  1. Get the complete court file. Go to the clerk of the court that entered the judgment and get the whole file — the complaint, and above all the affidavit of service. Everything turns on that document: who claims to have served you, where, when, and what the person served supposedly looked like. Note the judgment's date, amount, and docket/index number.
  2. Protect the money while you fight. A judgment creditor can keep collecting while your motion is pending unless the court stops it. A motion to vacate can ask the court to stay enforcement — the garnishment and the levy — while the service question is decided. Move fast: money taken is harder to recover than money frozen.
  3. Move to vacate for lack of personal jurisdiction. The ground is improper service of process: you were never served, so the court never acquired jurisdiction over you, so the judgment is void. In federal court, the vehicle is Rule 60(b)(4) — "the judgment is void" — which carries no ordinary one-year clock, because a void judgment is a nullity. In New York, the motion is brought under CPLR 5015(a)(4), the lack-of-jurisdiction ground. Your sworn affidavit is the centerpiece: state specifically that you were never served, never received the papers, and where you actually lived and were on the claimed date — attaching the proof you found (leases, time records, travel records).
  4. Win the traverse hearing. If your sworn denial is specific, the court can order a traverse hearing — a hearing that tries the single question: was this defendant actually served? Now the burden moves. The plaintiff must prove service was made, and the process server can be called to testify and cross-examined on the affidavit: Where exactly were you standing? Who opened the door? What did they say? The server's own records get tested against the story — the logbook, and in New York City the GPS records licensed servers must create and keep for seven years. A false affidavit written at a kitchen table does not survive a server who cannot describe the building he swore he visited.
  5. Take the full remedy. If service falls, the judgment is vacated — wiped out, with the liens and executions built on it exposed with it, and money seized under it subject to being ordered back. And then the part the sewer was built to prevent: if the plaintiff wants its debt, it must start over with real service — this time with you watching, answering, and defending on the merits, including defenses that were never heard the first time.
  6. Report the server. A false affidavit of service is a sworn lie filed in court. Report it to your state Attorney General's consumer-fraud bureau, and — in New York City — to the Department of Consumer and Worker Protection, which licenses process servers. Courts cannot police affidavits they never see challenged; your traverse hearing puts the lie on a public record.
The sentence to remember

To vacate a default judgment, never served means never notified — and never notified means the court never had you. The fight is not "I don't owe it." The fight is "this court never acquired me," and the affidavit is where that fight is won.

The enforcement record: New York took on the sewer

Sewer service is not a theory; it is a prosecuted crime pattern, and its capital was New York consumer-debt court. The record, from the New York Attorney General's own announcements:

April 2009 — the criminal case. Attorney General Andrew Cuomo announced criminal charges against Long Island-based American Legal Process and its president, William Singler, for a scheme in which the company failed to serve New Yorkers facing debt lawsuits and falsified sworn affidavits of service in courts across New York. The AG's papers stated the company claimed to have served 98,000 summonses and complaints between January 2007 and October 2008. Singler was charged with, among other crimes, criminal possession of a forged instrument — and in January 2010 he pleaded guilty to scheme to defraud in the first degree.

July 2009 — the 100,000 judgments. The Attorney General then sued 35 law firms and two debt collectors, asking the court to throw out an estimated 100,000 default judgments obtained in cases served by American Legal Process where the firms could show no proof of service beyond the company's affidavits. The average judgment was about $5,474 — real money, taken from people who never knew they had been sued. One firm alone had used the company for more than 28,000 services.

The scale around it. A New York City Bar Association report on the process-service industry put the backdrop in numbers: in 2009, 241,195 consumer credit cases were filed in New York City, and 66 percent ended in default judgments. When two out of three cases end with nobody answering, the affidavit of service stops being a formality and becomes the whole ballgame — which is exactly what the sewer operators understood.

2010 — the system answers. New York City enacted Local Law 7 of 2010, aimed squarely at sewer service: licensed process servers must carry an electronic device using GPS technology recording the time, date, and location of every service and attempt; keep logbooks and electronic records (city law now requires those records be kept for seven years); pass a licensing exam; carry a surety bond; and report traverse hearings to the Department of Consumer Affairs, which can investigate and discipline. The logic is the logic of this whole page: make the affidavit checkable against a record the server cannot rewrite at his kitchen table.

What this fight is — and what it is not

Not every default judgment is sewer service. Most defaults are what they appear to be: the defendant was served, did not answer, and lost by silence. An affidavit of service carries a presumption of validity in most courts, and a bare "I never got anything" — unsworn, unspecific, unproven — usually loses. The defendants who win traverse hearings win with specific sworn denials plus records: proof of where they lived, where they were, and what the affidavit got wrong.

Vacatur erases the judgment, not the debt. If the money is genuinely owed, the creditor can serve you properly and sue again in the open — and win on the merits. What you are recovering is your day in court: the right to answer, to demand proof, to raise the age of the debt and every other defense. That right is worth everything. It is not a debt-erasing machine, and anyone who sells it as one is running the same kind of con as the sewer server, from the other direction.

Rules vary by court and state. Deadlines, forms, and the exact vacatur standard differ — New York's CPLR 5015(a)(4) and the federal Rule 60(b)(4) are the examples used here because the sewer-service record is New York's. If a judgment is actively taking your wages, that is a live emergency with a clock on it: a licensed attorney in your state can file the right motion in the right court the first time.

Sewer service FAQ

What is sewer service?

It is the practice of a process server failing to serve the summons and complaint — the papers are discarded, in the old phrase, "down the sewer" — and then filing a sworn affidavit of service falsely stating the papers were delivered. The court file looks complete, the defendant never learns of the suit, the answer window runs out, and a default judgment is entered.

How do I vacate a default judgment if I was never served?

Get the complete court file, starting with the affidavit of service. Then move to vacate the judgment for lack of personal jurisdiction based on improper service of process — in federal court under Rule 60(b)(4), because a judgment entered without valid service is void; in New York under CPLR 5015(a)(4). A specific, sworn denial of service can earn a traverse hearing, where the court decides whether service actually happened.

What is a traverse hearing?

A hearing at which the court tries the single question of whether service of process was actually made. The process server can be called to testify and cross-examined on the affidavit of service, and the supporting records — the server's logbook and, in New York City, GPS records that must be kept for seven years — can be tested against the sworn statement.

What are the signs of a false affidavit of service?

Service claimed at an address where you never lived; a physical description of the person "served" that does not match you; service at a time you can prove you were elsewhere — at work, in a hospital, in another state; the same server claiming to have served implausibly many people across a wide area in the same hour; and boilerplate details that do not fit the actual building or household.

Does vacating the judgment erase the debt?

No. Vacatur erases the judgment, not the underlying claim. The plaintiff must start over with real service, and you finally get to defend the case on its merits — including defenses that were never heard the first time. What vacatur restores is the thing sewer service stole: your day in court.

What did New York do about sewer service?

In 2009 the New York Attorney General brought criminal charges against American Legal Process and its owner for falsifying affidavits of service — the company claimed to have served 98,000 summonses and complaints — and sued 35 law firms and two debt collectors to throw out an estimated 100,000 default judgments. The owner later pleaded guilty to scheme to defraud. New York City then required licensed process servers to carry GPS devices and keep electronic records of every service.

Bottom line

A court's power over you begins with an event — valid service — and sewer service is the counterfeiting of that event. The counterfeit works only as long as nobody reads the affidavit. Read it. If the delivery never happened, the judgment is void, the traverse hearing is your stage, and the case starts over in the light — which is where it should have started in the first place.
