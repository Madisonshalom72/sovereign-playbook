The Indian Citizenship Act of 1924: What It Actually Says — and What It Doesn't

The #1 Source for Information for Freemen in America

A video is going around with a simple pitch: an act from 1924 proves that people classified as "Black" in America are really Indigenous, and that a "status correction" filing can set the record — and the law — straight. The pitch names a real law. This article does what the video doesn't: it prints the law, all of it, and then checks every claim built on top of it.

The Act itself — the whole law is one sentence

The law's correct name is the Indian Citizenship Act of 1924, also called the Snyder Act after its sponsor, Rep. Homer P. Snyder. It is Public Law 68-175, printed at 43 Stat. 253, signed June 2, 1924, and its rule is carried today at 8 U.S.C. § 1401(b). No law on the books is officially named the "Indigenous Citizenship Act." Here is the complete operative text, exactly as enacted:

“That all non-citizen Indians born within the territorial limits of the United States be, and they are hereby, declared to be citizens of the United States: Provided, That the granting of such citizenship shall not in any manner impair or otherwise affect the right of any Indian to tribal or other property.”

That is the entire grant. One class of people — non-citizen Indians born within the United States — declared citizens, with their tribal and property rights expressly preserved. Read it as many times as the claim requires: the sentence does the work, and nothing else is hiding in it.

What the Act actually did

By 1924, roughly two-thirds of Native people in the United States already held citizenship through other doors — land allotment under the Dawes Act, military service, treaties, marriage, or state routes. The Snyder Act closed the gap for the rest: about 125,000 people out of a Native population of roughly 300,000. It was a genuine landmark, pushed over the line in part by Native service in the First World War.

Two honest limits belong in the same paragraph. First, the Act granted citizenship but not the vote by itself — the states still set voter qualifications, and Arizona and New Mexico kept Native citizens from voting until court rulings in 1948; Utah held out until 1957. Second, the Act did not enroll anyone in a tribe. Tribal citizenship stayed exactly where it had always been: with the tribes. A person could hold U.S. citizenship and tribal citizenship at once — dual citizenship — but the Act conferred only the first.

What the Act never mentions

The video's argument needs the Act to say things it does not say. In the full text of the law there is no mention of race reclassification, of "Black," Negro, colored, or mulatto, of African descent, of the census or vital records, of taxes, licenses, courts, or jurisdiction — and no filing, affidavit, or "correction" procedure of any kind. The National Archives, Congress.gov, and the Capitol Visitor Center all describe the law the same way: a grant of citizenship to non-citizen Indians. Nothing more was enacted, so nothing more can be quoted.

The other 1924 law — the one that really did rewrite records

Here is the true history the claim is standing on, and it deserves to be told straight. The same year, Virginia passed its Racial Integrity Act of 1924 — a state law, not the federal citizenship act. Under it, Registrar of Vital Statistics Walter Plecker spent three decades reclassifying Virginia's Indians as "colored" on birth certificates and public records, amending documents by hand and insisting that no Virginia Indian free of African ancestry existed. The National Park Service calls what happened a "paper genocide": communities like the Pamunkey, Mattaponi, Chickahominy, and Monacan had their recorded identity bureaucratically erased. On the 1940 census, one Virginia woman's entry shows "Ind" scratched out and replaced with "colored."

So yes — labels on records were imposed, shifted, and falsified, and real families carry the scars of it. But hold the finding to its size: the documented cases are specific, persistent tribal communities whose records a specific state official rewrote. No historian and no federal source concludes from it that Black Americans generally are Indigenous rather than of African descent. A true wound in one place is not a new identity for everyone.

Three separate doors — the Act opened only one

The pitch blends three different things into one. Keep them separate and the whole question clears up:

1. U.S. citizenship — federal, granted by Congress under the Constitution. This is the only door the 1924 Act touched.
2. Race written on records — state registrars and census enumerators, the Plecker machinery. Governed by state law and practice — the federal Act said nothing about it.
3. Tribal citizenship — each sovereign tribe's own decision, under its own law, on documented descent. The Supreme Court confirmed in Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez (1978) that membership is the tribe's to define. The Bureau of Indian Affairs states it plainly: tribal enrollment "is determined and set by individual Tribes, not the Bureau of Indian Affairs."

A change at one door does not rewrite the others — and a filing at a county recorder's office opens none of them. A recorder archives paper; the archive stamp is not a ruling.

What the courts say about "status correction"

The filing theory has been tested, repeatedly, and it has lost every time. In Bey v. Indiana, 847 F.3d 559 (7th Cir. 2017), a man describing himself as an "Aboriginal Indigenous Moorish-American" asked the court to block Indiana's taxes on the strength of his claimed status. The Seventh Circuit answered that his claims were "without any basis in fact," and wrote the sentence that ends every version of this case: "He is not a sovereign citizen. He is a U.S. citizen," with no immunity from U.S. law — and the court noted he was lucky to be spared sanctions. The same court had already ruled in United States v. Benabe, 654 F.3d 753 (7th Cir. 2011): "Regardless of an individual's claimed status of descent… that person is not beyond the jurisdiction of the courts. These theories should be rejected summarily." Affidavit-based filings fare no better at the IRS, where a frivolous submission can draw a $5,000 penalty under 26 U.S.C. § 6702 — and the tax and the levy stand anyway.

The lawful door that does exist

None of this means ancestry is fiction or that every claim is a scam. Some Black families hold documented Native descent, and where the documents run, the door is real. The Cherokee Nation — the tribe named in the button under that video — grants citizenship to anyone who can trace direct lineal descent from an original enrollee on the Dawes Rolls (1898–1906), with certified records, generation by generation. There is no blood-quantum minimum — and descendants of the Cherokee Freedmen qualify on the same documented terms, a right confirmed in Cherokee Nation v. Nash (2017) and by the Cherokee Nation Supreme Court in 2021. But the tribe is equally plain about what does not work: "DNA results, family photos, and resources found through genealogy websites are not valid proof." The application goes to the tribe's Registration Department — not to a recorder, not to the BIA, and not by affidavit of self-declaration.

Questions, answered plainly

Did the Indian Citizenship Act of 1924 reclassify Black Americans as Indigenous?
No. The Act is one sentence granting U.S. citizenship to non-citizen Indians born in the United States. It mentions no other group and no reclassification of anyone.

Were Native people's records really rewritten as "colored"?
Yes — in Virginia, under the state's separate Racial Integrity Act of 1924, Registrar Walter Plecker systematically reclassified Virginia Indians as "colored" on vital records. Historians and the National Park Service document it as a "paper genocide" against specific tribal communities.

Can a "status correction" affidavit change my legal status?
No court has accepted one. Recorders only archive documents, the BIA does not enroll or reclassify anyone, and courts from the Seventh Circuit down reject status-based exemptions from taxes, licenses, and jurisdiction — sometimes with sanctions or a $5,000 frivolous-filing penalty.

How does someone actually become a Cherokee citizen?
By the Cherokee Nation's own process: prove direct descent from a Dawes Rolls enrollee with certified documents, and apply through the tribe's Registration Department. DNA tests and self-declared affidavits are not accepted.

Did the 1924 Act give Native people the right to vote?
Not by itself. States controlled voter qualifications; Arizona and New Mexico barred Native voting until 1948 court decisions, and Utah until 1957.

The bottom line

A real one-sentence law. A real history of records falsified against Native people. And then a leap that the Act's text, the federal courts, and the tribes themselves all refuse to make. The Freeman's Journal prints the sentence and the record together, because a reader holding both cannot be sold a shortcut — and the lawful doors, genealogy and the tribe's own rolls, are still standing open for the families whose documents truly run there.
