How to File a UCC-1 Financing Statement: Step-by-Step

By Madison Hoskins • UCC Talk24 • Updated October 13, 2026 • Educational guide — not legal advice

A UCC-1 financing statement is how a secured party gives public notice of its interest in a debtor's personal property. It looks like a simple one-page form — and that's exactly why people get it wrong. One wrong letter in the debtor's name, the wrong filing office, or a missed 5-year deadline can make the whole filing worthless. This guide walks you through filing a UCC-1 correctly, step by step, with the actual UCC sections that govern each step.

What a UCC-1 is — and what it is not

The UCC-1 is a notice filing under Article 9 of the Uniform Commercial Code. It tells the world: "this secured party claims a security interest in described collateral of this debtor." Lenders file UCC-1s every day to protect equipment loans, inventory financing, factored invoices, and other secured deals.

Understand what the form does not do, because this is where most internet misinformation starts:

  • A UCC-1 does not create a security interest by itself. The interest comes from a security agreement the debtor signs. The filing only perfects it — it establishes your place in line against other creditors.
  • A UCC-1 does not discharge debts, taxes, or court judgments, and it is not a lien you place on the government or on a "strawman." Filings made without a real underlying secured transaction can be rejected, and some states impose penalties for fraudulent or unauthorized filings.
  • A UCC-1 does not last forever. It lapses after 5 years unless you continue it (§9-515).

Before you file: the two prerequisites

1. A real security agreement. The debtor must have rights in the collateral and must sign (authenticate) a security agreement describing it. Under §9-509, by authenticating a security agreement the debtor authorizes the secured party to file a financing statement covering the collateral described. No agreement, no authorized filing.

2. The debtor's exact legal name and location. Two facts drive everything else on the form: the debtor's legally correct name (§9-503) and the debtor's location (§9-307), which decides which state's filing office you use. Get both before you touch the form.

How to file a UCC-1 in 7 steps

  1. Get the debtor's exact legal name. For a business (a "registered organization"), use the name exactly as it appears on its public organic record — the articles of organization or incorporation on file with the state, including "LLC," "Inc.," punctuation, and spacing. For an individual, most states follow the driver's-license rule: use the name exactly as shown on the debtor's unexpired driver's license or state ID. Do not use trade names, DBAs, nicknames, or shortened versions (§9-503).
  2. Identify the secured party. Enter the secured party's legal name and mailing address. If the loan is later sold or assigned, this is the party of record a searcher will find.
  3. Describe the collateral. The financing statement only needs to reasonably identify the collateral (§9-108) — by category, type, quantity, or specific listing. "All equipment" or "all inventory and equipment" is acceptable on the financing statement. (Note the difference: in the security agreement, a supergeneric "all assets" description is not sufficient under §9-108(c) — describe it properly there.) If the filing covers crops, timber, minerals, or fixtures, say so and use the addendum fields.
  4. Choose the correct filing office. The default rule (§9-501) is central filing with the Secretary of State of the state where the debtor is located — not where the collateral sits. Debtor location (§9-307): an individual is located at their principal residence; a registered organization is located in its state of organization; other organizations at their chief executive office. The exception: fixture filings and filings for timber, minerals, or oil and gas are filed in the local real-estate recording office of the county where the property is.
  5. Complete the current national UCC-1 form. Most states accept the national form (rev. 2023) or their state version, and most offer online filing portals that are faster and cheaper than paper. Online systems also catch missing fields before you submit — use them where available.
  6. Pay the fee and submit. Fees are set by each state and vary by method and page count; a standard filing typically runs about $10–$25. Keep the payment confirmation.
  7. Verify the filing and calendar the deadline. After filing, run a UCC search against the debtor's exact legal name in the same system and confirm your filing appears, spelled exactly as intended — this is the same search a lender's lawyer will run later. Save the file-stamped copy or acknowledgment with the file number, then calendar two dates: the 5-year lapse date, and the date 6 months before it, when your continuation window opens.

Need more room? The UCC-1Ad (Addendum) handles fixture/real-estate details and additional collateral, and the UCC-1Ap (Additional Party) adds more debtors or secured parties. Amendments, continuations, assignments, and terminations all go on the UCC-3 form, referencing the original file number.

5 mistakes that make a UCC filing worthless

1. The debtor's name is wrong

This is the #1 killer. A name error that is seriously misleading makes the filing ineffective (§9-506). The practical test: if a searcher running the filing office's standard search logic under the debtor's correct name would not find your filing, the error is seriously misleading. A missing "LLC," a transposed letter in a surname, or a nickname instead of the license name can each be fatal.

2. Filing in the wrong office

Filing where the collateral is located instead of where the debtor is located is the classic beginner error. File in the debtor's state (central filing), except for fixture and extracted-collateral filings, which go to the local land records (§9-501, §9-307).

3. Letting the filing lapse

A UCC-1 dies after 5 years (§9-515). The continuation statement can only be filed within the 6 months before the lapse date — not earlier, and not one day after. Miss the window and the filing is ineffective; if you re-file, you go to the back of the priority line with a new date.

4. A collateral description that doesn't do its job

Too vague in the security agreement ("all my stuff," or a bare supergeneric phrase) fails §9-108(c); sloppy in a different way — describing collateral the debtor never agreed to — creates an unauthorized-filing problem. The financing statement and the security agreement have different description standards. Know which document you're drafting.

5. Filing without authorization

Under §9-509, a filing is only effective if the debtor authorized it — normally through the signed security agreement. Filing "just in case," before the agreement exists, or against someone you have no real secured transaction with, exposes you to statutory damages under §9-625 and, in many states, criminal or civil penalties for fraudulent filings.

After filing: continuation, amendment, termination

Continuation (UCC-3). File within the 6-month window before the 5-year lapse date. A timely continuation extends the filing for another 5 years from the original lapse date (§9-515(d)–(e)). You can repeat this every 5 years indefinitely.

Amendment (UCC-3). Use it to add or delete collateral, add or change a debtor or secured party, or change addresses. Note: an amendment cannot fix a seriously misleading debtor name retroactively for collateral acquired more than 4 months after the original filing in some situations — another reason to get the name right on day one.

Assignment (UCC-3). When a loan is sold, an assignment puts the new secured party of record on the public record.

Termination (UCC-3). When the obligation is satisfied, the secured party files (or authorizes) a termination statement so the debtor's record is clear (§9-513). For consumer goods, the secured party generally must file or send a termination within a month after there's no longer any outstanding obligation.

Debtor moved or reorganized? If the debtor changes its name, structure, or location, your filing can become seriously misleading as to new collateral after 4 months (name/identity changes) — and a move to a new jurisdiction gives you 1 year of continued perfection for existing collateral. Amend promptly (§9-316, §9-507, §9-508).

UCC-1 filing FAQ

How much does it cost to file a UCC-1?

Fees are set by each state and vary by method (online vs. paper) and page count. Most states charge roughly $10–$25 for a standard one- or two-page UCC-1 through the Secretary of State. Check your filing office's current schedule before submitting.

How long does a UCC-1 filing last?

Five years from the filing date (§9-515). File a continuation (UCC-3) within the 6 months before it lapses to extend it another 5 years. A lapsed filing is ineffective — and priority can be lost permanently.

Where do I file a UCC-1 financing statement?

Centrally, with the Secretary of State (or equivalent) of the state where the debtor is located — except fixture filings and timber/mineral/oil-and-gas filings, which are recorded locally with the land records.

What name goes on the UCC-1 for the debtor?

For a business: the exact name on its public organic record. For an individual: in most states, the exact name on the unexpired driver's license or state ID. Minor errors that are not seriously misleading may survive under §9-506 — name errors that defeat a standard search do not.

Can I file a UCC-1 on myself?

A financing statement exists to perfect a security interest created by a security agreement between a debtor and a secured party, and the debtor must authorize the filing (§9-509). A UCC-1 by itself creates nothing, discharges nothing, and filings without a real secured transaction behind them can be rejected or penalized. If you are both the business owner and the lender in a genuine transaction (for example, your holding company lending to your operating company), document the real agreement first — and get advice on your specific structure.

Bottom line

Filing a UCC-1 is a 15-minute task with 5-year consequences. Exact debtor name, correct office, a collateral description that matches the security agreement, and a calendared continuation date — do those four things and your filing will do the job it exists for.
