UCC Article 12, Explained: The Controllable Electronic Record in Plain Language

By Madison Hoskins • UCC Talk24 • Published November 16, 2026 • Educational guide — not legal advice

In 2022 the Uniform Commercial Code added a whole new article for digital assets — and almost everything written about it since is law-firm alerts written for other lawyers. This is UCC Article 12 explained for the rest of us. Its centerpiece is the controllable electronic record: a digital asset the law finally treats the way it has always treated a paper instrument. Below: what it is (§12-102), what control of a controllable electronic record means (§12-105), why control beats filing (§9-326A), what a qualifying purchaser gets (§12-104) — and the Pennsylvania angle, because Article 12 Pennsylvania is not coming someday. It is already law.

What is a controllable electronic record?

Start with the problem. Before 2022, the Code had no shelf for a bitcoin or a token. A digital asset was a "general intangible" — the Code's leftover drawer for property you cannot touch. You cannot possess an intangible, so the old law of instruments, built on holding the paper, did not fit. A lender could file a financing statement, but a filing told a buyer nothing about whether he took the asset clean or took it subject to a stranger's claim. Commerce ran on platform terms and hope.

Article 12 — approved in 2022 by the American Law Institute and the Uniform Law Commission as the centerpiece of the 2022 UCC amendments — builds the missing shelf. The definition, §12-102(a)(1), word for word:

§12-102(a)(1) — the definition

"'Controllable electronic record' means a record stored in an electronic medium that can be subjected to control under Section 12-105."

Notice what the definition does not name: no blockchain, no token standard, no technology at all. It asks a functional question — can this record be subjected to control? — so the article can govern machines not yet invented. The exclusions are a map of the rest of the Code: a controllable electronic record is not a controllable account, not a controllable payment intangible, not a deposit account, not an electronic copy of a record evidencing chattel paper, not an electronic document of title, not electronic money, not investment property, and not a transferable record. Each of those already has its own article and its own rules. Article 12 takes what is left — bitcoin, ether, most tokens and NFTs held directly.

Control: possession, rebuilt for a record

Every instrument system in history runs on one question: who holds it? For paper, the answer was possession. For a record, §12-105(a) rebuilds possession as control. A person has control of a controllable electronic record when the record, a record attached or logically associated with it, or the system it is recorded in gives that person four things:

  1. The power to avail itself of substantially all the benefit from the record — to use it, spend it, enjoy it.
  2. The exclusive power to prevent others from availing themselves of substantially all that benefit.
  3. The exclusive power to transfer control of the record to someone else.
  4. The ability readily to identify itself as the person holding those powers — by name, identifying number, cryptographic key, office, or account number.

Benefit, exclusion, transfer, identity — read those four against a private key and the machine shows through the statute: the practical facts of holding a key, written as law. Two softeners matter. Under §12-105(b), the powers stay "exclusive" even if the system limits how the record can be used, or the power is shared under the record's own rules. And under §12-105(e), you can hold control through another person — a custodian — who acknowledges that he holds it on your behalf. A screenshot of a wallet is not control. A password the other party also keeps is not control. Control means the powers are yours, exclusively — or held by someone who has acknowledged, in a record, that he holds them for you.

Why control beats filing

Article 9's old rhythm was first-to-file. Article 12 breaks it on purpose. A security interest in a controllable electronic record can be perfected two ways — by filing a financing statement, or by taking control. When the two collide, §9-326A awards the race to the key:

§9-326A — the priority rule

A security interest in a controllable electronic record held by a secured party having control has priority over a conflicting security interest held by a secured party that does not have control — even if the filing came first.

And one sentence in §12-104(h) shows how deliberate the design is: "Filing of a financing statement under Article 9 is not notice of a claim of a property right in a controllable electronic record." The filing office does not poison a buyer's good faith. A lender who files and sleeps, in this asset class, is a lender preparing to be second. The Code wants these records to circulate like instruments — so it protects the people who take them the way instruments are taken: by holding them.

The qualifying purchaser takes it clean

Now the prize. §12-102(a)(2) defines the qualifying purchaser: a purchaser who obtains control of the record for value, in good faith, and without notice of a claim of a property right in it. And §12-104(e) pays him — a qualifying purchaser "acquires its rights in the controllable electronic record free of a claim of a property right in the controllable electronic record."

You have met this man before. He is the holder in due course of Article 3 — the buyer of a negotiable note who takes it free of most claims and defenses because he gave value, acted in good faith, and had no notice — rebuilt for records. Value, good faith, no notice, and now control instead of possession. That is the whole architecture of Article 12 in one line: §12-102 says what it is, §12-105 says what holding it means, §12-104 says what the honest buyer gets.

What Article 12 changes: crypto, tokenized notes, digital assets

Crypto held directly

Bitcoin or ether you hold yourself is now a defined asset class with defined rules in enacting states: it can be collateral perfected by control, and it can be sold to a qualifying purchaser who takes it free of property claims. Before Article 12, both of those statements ran on general-intangible leftovers and argument.

Tokenized notes and invoices

Keep the note as the agreement, but evidence its payment right with a controllable electronic record in which the maker undertakes to pay the person who has control of the record — and that payment right becomes a controllable payment intangible (§9-102(a)(27B)); for an invoice, a controllable account (§9-102(a)(27A)). The payoff is the qualifying-purchaser rule working on the payment right itself: the buyer who takes control takes the right to be paid, free of property claims — the holder-in-due-course result, delivered through the token. One discipline never changes, and this site will keep repeating it: the token is the record; the agreement is the right. A token pointing at nothing — no note, no undertaking, no agreement — is a portrait sold as the sitter.

Assets held on platforms

Much of the market never holds a record directly. When a platform's terms say you granted it "all right and title" to your coins, a bankruptcy court will read the agreement, not the app screen — the Celsius Earn customers learned that in 2023, when roughly $4.2 billion of customer crypto was ruled property of the bankruptcy estate because the terms they accepted said so. Article 12 governs records you control. What an intermediary holds for you lives on a different shelf — Article 8 securities entitlements — and the first professional question in any digital-asset deal is which shelf the asset is actually sitting on.

Article 12 in Pennsylvania: Act 41 of 2024

Article 12 is state law or it is nothing — the UCC is a model code, and it governs only where a state has enacted it. The map, stated with its date because it moves: the 2022 amendments have been enacted in 36 states and the District of Columbia, as of October 2026 (Uniform Law Commission enactment table). New York's enactment took effect June 3, 2026. In a state that has not enacted, Article 12 does not exist as law, and the old general-intangible rules still run. Never say "Article 12 provides" without naming the state whose statute you are reading.

For Pennsylvania, there is nothing to wait for. Pennsylvania enacted the 2022 amendments as Act 41 of 2024, effective August 30, 2024 — and Article 12 sits in Pennsylvania law as Division 12 of Title 13 of the Pennsylvania Consolidated Statutes (13 Pa.C.S.). One timing note for lenders: each enacting state set an adjustment date until which filing-only interests perfected before the amendments keep their old priority; Pennsylvania's adjustment date was August 30, 2025, and it has passed — the new control-first rule now runs in full.

Where to read the text — an honest note

Cornell Law Institute's Legal Information Institute — the free source this site links for most UCC articles — does not host Article 12. To read the words themselves, go to the enacted statute of an adopting state (for Pennsylvania, Division 12 of 13 Pa.C.S.) or the official 2022 amendments published by the Uniform Law Commission and the American Law Institute. Any site quoting "Article 12" without naming a state's enacted text is quoting a draft, not a law.

Honest limits

Article 12 is new law — enacted in most states only in the last few years, with little case law testing it yet, and it does not exist at all in states that have not enacted the 2022 amendments. It also does not make any token a good investment, validate any platform's promises, or turn a badly drafted deal into a good one: the agreement still creates the right, and securities laws still govern interests sold as investments. This page teaches the structure. For a transaction that matters, have a licensed attorney in your state review the documents before you sign.

Article 12 FAQ

What is a controllable electronic record?

Under §12-102(a)(1), a record stored in an electronic medium that can be subjected to control under §12-105. In plain language: a digital asset — bitcoin, ether, most tokens and NFTs held directly — that one person can hold, exclude others from, and transfer, the way you would hold a paper instrument.

What does control mean under §12-105?

The record or its system gives you the power to get substantially all its benefit, the exclusive power to keep others from that benefit, the exclusive power to transfer control, and the ability to identify yourself as the holder — by name, number, cryptographic key, office, or account number. You can also hold control through a custodian who acknowledges holding it for you (§12-105(e)).

Does filing a UCC-1 protect me in a digital-asset deal?

Filing perfects a security interest, but it loses to control: under §9-326A a secured party with control has priority over one who only filed, and under §12-104(h) a filing is not even notice of a claim against a qualifying purchaser. In this asset class, filing is the backup — control is the play.

Is UCC Article 12 law in Pennsylvania?

Yes. Pennsylvania enacted the 2022 amendments as Act 41 of 2024, effective August 30, 2024; Article 12 is Division 12 of 13 Pa.C.S. As of October 2026, the amendments had been enacted in 36 states and the District of Columbia.

Where can I read the text of Article 12?

Not at Cornell LII — it does not host Article 12. Read the enacted statute of an adopting state (Pennsylvania: Division 12 of 13 Pa.C.S.) or the official 2022 amendments from the Uniform Law Commission and the American Law Institute.

Bottom line

Commerce invented the token first; the Code arrived in 2022 to give it rules. Definition, control, clean title — §12-102 says what it is, §12-105 says what holding it means, §12-104 says what the honest buyer gets, and §9-326A says the key beats the filing cabinet. In Pennsylvania, that is governing law today.
