How to File a UCC-1: The Honest Step-by-Step Guide

By Madison Hoskins • UCC Talk24 • Published November 16, 2026 • Educational guide — not legal advice

Motion graphic — animated, never a static picture.

A UCC-1 financing statement is one of the most misunderstood forms in American commerce. It is not a money machine, a status upgrade, or a way to erase a debt. It is a short public notice that says one plain thing: a named secured party claims a security interest in a named debtor's described collateral, under a real agreement. This guide walks through the honest process in order, so you can file correctly, avoid the mistakes that kill filings, and recognise the pitches that misuse this form.

What a UCC-1 financing statement is

A UCC-1 is the financing statement used under Article 9 of the Uniform Commercial Code. When a lender, a seller on credit, or another secured party takes a security interest in a debtor's property, the UCC-1 is the form filed in the public record to announce that interest to the world. Think of it as a flag on the public record, not the deal itself. The deal is the security agreement. The filing is the notice. That distinction matters, because the filing office does not check whether your deal is real, fair, or wise. In most states the office is ministerial: if the form is facially complete, it is accepted and indexed. Acceptance is not approval, and it is not proof that a valid security interest exists. A filing that announces a fake interest is still a filing on the record — and it can still get the filer into serious trouble. The honest use is narrow and practical. A bank lends money to buy equipment and files to protect its priority against other creditors. A supplier sells goods on credit and files so a later lender cannot claim the same goods first. A business owner who lends to their own company documents the loan and files. In each case, there is a real transaction, real value, and real collateral. The UCC-1 simply makes that claim visible, so anyone searching the debtor's name can find it before they lend, buy, or take a lien.

First: the security agreement comes first

Before you touch a filing form, you need a security agreement. Under UCC §9-203, a security interest attaches — that is, it becomes enforceable against the debtor — only when three things are true. First, value has been given: money lent, goods sold on credit, or some other real consideration. Second, the debtor has rights in the collateral: you cannot grant a security interest in property you do not own or have rights in. Third, the debtor has authenticated a security agreement that describes the collateral. Authenticated means signed, or otherwise agreed to in a record the debtor adopted. Without those three, there is nothing to perfect. Filing first and hoping the agreement will follow is backwards. It is also how people end up with a filing on the record and no enforceable right behind it. Put the agreement in writing, keep it signed and dated, and describe the collateral clearly enough that a stranger reading it later can tell what is covered. State the amount, the payment terms, and what happens on default. If you cannot write that agreement in plain language, you are not ready to file. Get help from a licensed attorney before you create a record that will follow you, and the debtor, for five years. The filing only perfects an interest that already exists. Perfection is about priority: it puts later searchers on notice and, in most cases, holds your place in line ahead of later filers and lien creditors. It does not create value, it does not transfer ownership, and it does not turn a promise into a payment. A filed UCC-1 with no agreement behind it is a notice of nothing.

Step 1: Get the debtor's exact legal name

The most important field on the form is the debtor's name, and it is the one people get wrong most often. The rule is strict for a reason: the whole public-notice system depends on searchers being able to find the filing by searching the debtor's name. Under UCC §9-506, a financing statement that is seriously misleading as to the debtor's name is ineffective. For an individual debtor, that generally means the name as it appears on their unexpired driver's license, or, if they have none, the name on their state-issued identification. Do not use a nickname, a shortened first name, a maiden name, or a trade name in the debtor-name field. For a registered organisation, use the exact name on its charter documents — the name as filed with the state of organisation — including the entity ending, such as LLC or Inc., exactly as registered. Copy it letter for letter, and check the spelling against the source document, not from memory. If the debtor is a trust, an estate, or another entity, the naming rules are different and more detailed, so do not guess. A filing under the wrong name may never be found by the searcher it was meant to warn, and a court can treat it as if it were never filed. That is an expensive lesson to learn after a default, when priority is decided by the record. Take five minutes with the license or charter in hand. It is the cheapest insurance in this whole process.

Step 2: Describe the collateral

Next, describe the collateral — the property the security interest covers. The description in the financing statement can be broader than the description in the security agreement, but it still has to be reasonable. A description that says only “all assets” can be sufficient in a financing statement in many states, but a vague description in the security agreement itself is a problem, because the agreement is what creates the interest. Be specific where you can be specific. If the collateral is equipment, list the type, make, model, and serial number. If it is inventory, say so, and say whether after-acquired inventory is included. If it is accounts receivable, describe them as accounts, and be clear about whether future accounts are covered. Do not claim collateral you have no right to claim. A lender who files on “all assets” when the agreement covers only one truck has created a mismatch that will cause trouble later, whether in a dispute with another creditor or in court. Keep the two documents consistent. The agreement should describe the collateral in detail, and the filing should match the agreement's scope. Keep a copy of both, and keep the serial numbers, invoices, and photographs that prove what the collateral is. When the debtor later sells, trades, or replaces that property, update the record if the agreement requires it. A filing is a snapshot, not a living document, and it only protects what it actually describes.

Step 3: Choose the correct filing office

Under UCC §9-501, a standard financing statement is filed centrally, with the filing office of the state where the debtor is located. For most debtors, that means the Secretary of State, or the equivalent central filing office. In Pennsylvania, that office is the Department of State. For a registered organisation, the debtor is located in its state of organisation. For an individual, the debtor is located in their state of principal residence. Filing in the wrong state, or in a county office when central filing is required, leaves the interest unperfected even if the form is perfect in every other way. There are exceptions — fixtures, timber, and minerals can involve local real-property records — but the ordinary commercial filing goes to the central state office. Check the filing office's own website before you file. Most states now accept filings online, and the online system will walk you through the debtor name, secured party, and collateral fields. Use the state's own portal, not a third-party service that charges a large markup to submit the same form. If you are filing on a debtor located in another state, file in that state. Do not file where you happen to live, or where the collateral happens to sit, unless the debtor is located there. Location follows the debtor, not the property.

Step 4: File and pay the fee

Once the agreement is signed, the name is exact, the collateral is described, and the office is right, file the financing statement and pay the fee. The fee is set by each state, and it varies widely. Pennsylvania charges a flat $84 to file a financing statement with the Department of State, regardless of pages or number of debtors. Other states are much cheaper: Texas charges $5 for a standard filing, and North Carolina charges $38 for a one-to-two-page statement. There is no federal UCC office and no federal fee. Anyone charging you hundreds of dollars just to submit the form is adding a markup the state never sees. File online where the state allows it, pay by the method the state accepts, and save the acknowledgment. The filing office will return a confirmation or an acceptance notice, usually with a filing number and a filing date. Save that notice with the security agreement. It is your proof of when perfection happened, and the filing date is what later priority disputes turn on. Then search the record yourself, under the debtor's exact name, and confirm that the filing appears as you expect. If the name is misspelled in the index, or the collateral description is cut off, fix it now, while the filing is fresh. A filing you never verify is a filing you are only guessing about.

Step 5: Calendar the 5-year lapse date

A filed financing statement does not last forever. Under UCC §9-515, it is generally effective for five years from the filing date, then it lapses. A lapsed filing stops perfecting the interest, and priority can be lost to a later filer. The cure is a continuation statement — a UCC-3 — filed within the six months before the lapse date. Not a year before, and not the day after: within that six-month window. Put the lapse date on your calendar the day you file, and set reminders at twelve months, six months, and thirty days before it. If the loan is repaid, the collateral is sold, or the interest ends for another reason, the secured party should file a termination statement, so the public record stops showing a claim that no longer exists. Keep the whole file together: the signed security agreement, the filing acknowledgment, the search results, the continuation, and the termination. Five years is a long time, and people, businesses, and addresses change. The calendar entry is what keeps a good filing from quietly expiring while nobody is watching. If the debtor changes their name, moves to another state, or reorganises, the filing may need an amendment as well. Check the rules for that change when it happens, rather than assuming the old filing still covers the new situation.

Common mistakes

Most failed filings fail in one of a handful of ways. The debtor's name is wrong, shortened, or taken from a business card instead of a license or charter. The security agreement is missing, unsigned, or so vague about the collateral that it describes nothing. The filing goes to the wrong state, or to a county office that was never the right place. The fee is paid to a middleman who charges ten times the state price. The continuation is filed too early, too late, or never. Or the filer files against themselves, or against a person they have no transaction with, believing the filing itself creates a right. None of these is a small error. Each one can make the filing ineffective, or turn it into a liability. The fix for almost every one is the same: slow down, use the source documents, file at the state's own office, and keep the paper trail. If the transaction is large, or the collateral is unusual, have a licensed attorney review the agreement and the filing before you submit them. The cost of an hour of review is small next to the cost of losing priority in a bankruptcy, or defending a filing you should never have made.

Scam warning

The rule that never fails

“If they tell you to file a UCC-1 or a 1099-A to get money, it could possibly be a scam.”

That sentence is the simplest test in this whole subject. A UCC-1 moves no money, opens no account, and discharges no debt. If someone is selling you a package, a course, or a “process” that promises money in exchange for filing one, they are selling the filing, not the result. The same is true of Form 1099-A, which is an information return a lender files when it acquires secured property — not a form an individual files to claim funds.
