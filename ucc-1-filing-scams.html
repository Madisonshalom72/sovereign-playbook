UCC-1 Filing Scams: How the Traps Work — and How to Spot Them

By Madison Hoskins • UCC Talk24 • Published November 13, 2026 • Educational guide — not legal advice

It arrives in your mailbox looking like it came from the state: an official-style notice, your business name and filing details printed right on it, a deadline, and a fee — $90, $98, $107 — for a UCC document you have never heard of needing. UCC-1 filing scams feed on that one moment of uncertainty, and the letter in your hand is only the cheapest of the five traps built around this one simple form. This page breaks down each UCC filing scam — how it works, the tell that gives it away, and what to do instead — so you can keep your money and your record clean.

The 60-second truth: what a UCC-1 actually is

Every scam below depends on you not knowing this paragraph. A UCC-1 financing statement is a public notice form under Article 9 of the Uniform Commercial Code. A lender (the secured party) files it to announce: "I have a security interest in this debtor's described collateral, under a signed security agreement." That is all it does. It creates nothing by itself, moves no money, opens no account, and discharges no debt. The security interest comes from the security agreement; the filing just perfects it — it holds the lender's place in line. A legitimate filing also requires the debtor's authorization (§9-509), which normally comes from signing that agreement. No real loan, no signed agreement, no legitimate filing. Hold onto that, and every pitch that follows falls apart on contact. For the legitimate process, see our step-by-step guide: How to file a UCC-1 yourself.

Scam #1: The official-looking "UCC Statement Service" letter

How it works. UCC filings are public records. Solicitation companies scrape them, then mail the debtors listed on them a form designed to look like a government invoice — "UCC Statement Request Form," a response deadline, and a charge to send you a copy of "your" financing statement. Tennessee's Secretary of State Tre Hargett warned residents about the "TN UCC Statement Service" mailing, which charged $107 for a copy of a UCC-1 that the Secretary of State's own office provides for $15. The Texas Attorney General issued a consumer alert about the same play in Texas — $90 for copies available from the Texas Secretary of State, where a records search starts at $1. Ohio's Secretary of State warns that scam companies charge $90 or more for UCC statements you can download from the state's own UCC portal — in Ohio's case, free. North Carolina's Attorney General has warned business owners about the same mailings.

The tell. That letter is a fake UCC filing notice. The sender's name is vague — "UCC Statement Service," "UCC Processing Center" — never an actual agency. The price is many times the state's. And buried in the fine print, most of these mailers admit the truth: the sender "is not affiliated with any government agency." Real filing offices don't bill you by mail for documents you never ordered.

What to do instead. Throw it away — ignoring it carries no legal consequences. If you actually want a copy of a filing or a search of the record, go straight to your Secretary of State's UCC section (in Pennsylvania, the Department of State) and pay the state's price, not a middleman's markup. If the mailing is deceptive, your state Attorney General's consumer-protection office takes these complaints — the warnings above exist because people filed them.

Scam #2: The guru package — "become a secured party creditor"

How it works. Seminars, courses, and "kits" — sold for hundreds to thousands of dollars — teach a script: you have a secret second identity (a "strawman") created at birth, a hidden government account attached to it, and by filing a UCC-1 naming yourself as the secured party of your own name, you supposedly step into a magic legal status. Debts discharged. Taxes optional. Priority over every bank and agency. Marketers dress the same family of claims up as "redemption" or "commercial redemption" — the pitch researchers and the FBI track as the UCC redemption scam. The FBI has warned about these schemes for years, noting that adherents file legitimate IRS and UCC forms "for illegitimate purposes, believing that doing so correctly will compel the U.S. Treasury to fulfill its debts." The IRS publishes the same theories in its guidance on frivolous tax arguments.

The tell. Notice who gets paid first: the guru. The package fee is real; everything it promises is not. There is no strawman account, no Treasury balance with your name on it, and no filing that changes your legal status. Under Article 9 itself, an enforceable security interest requires value given, a debtor with rights in real collateral, and an authenticated security agreement (§9-203) — a filing against your own name satisfies none of it. Every court to consider the theory has rejected it as frivolous.

What to do instead. Walk away, and keep the package fee. If you genuinely lend money or sell on credit, becoming a real secured party is a legitimate, ordinary process — a signed security agreement, then a UCC-1 filed at the state office — and it is covered step by step in our UCC-1 filing guide. What no course can sell you is an exemption from your own debts.

Scam #3: The 1099-A scam — "file a form, get money"

The rule that never fails

"If they tell you to file a UCC-1 or a 1099-A to get money, it could possibly be a scam."

How it works. A coach or online promoter tells you the government is holding money in your name, and the "key" is paperwork: file a UCC-1 against yourself, or file IRS Form 1099-A — sometimes alongside its cousin, the 1099-OID — and refunds, mortgage payoffs, or spendable credit will flow. Some operations charged victims per filing to "transmit" the forms for them. The story changes costumes — secret account, birth-certificate bond, redemption — but the move is always the same: you file the form, they collect the fee.

The reality. Start with what the forms actually are. A UCC-1 is a notice of someone else's real security interest — it moves no money, ever. Form 1099-A, "Acquisition or Abandonment of Secured Property," is an information return a lender files when it acquires property securing a loan (for example, through foreclosure) or learns the property was abandoned; the borrower gets a copy for their tax records. It is not a claim form, and no individual files one to receive funds. The IRS names this exact play in Notice 2010-33, its official list of frivolous positions: using a Form 1099-OID or similar instrument to draw money from a supposed government "straw man" account is frivolous, and every frivolous submission carries a $5,000 penalty under 26 U.S.C. § 6702 — per filing, before any criminal exposure.

The enforcement record. This is not theory; it is a prosecuted crime pattern. In a case prosecuted by the U.S. Attorney for the Western District of Missouri, participants in a nationwide refund scheme attempted nearly $96 million in fraudulent refunds using fictitious 1099-OID forms — and two of the defendants admitted they had charged clients $200 per person or couple to transmit Forms 1099-OID and 1099-A through their company. In the Western District of Washington, promoter Ronald L. Brekke was sentenced to 12 years in federal prison for a scheme whose pitch was that the Treasury would pay refunds equal to a person's debts; his clients claimed over $763 million, and the IRS paid out roughly $14 million before shutting it down. The victims' ledger is always the same: guru fees gone, IRS penalties stacked on top of the original tax bill, refunds clawed back with restitution — and the mortgage, the cards, and the debts still owed in full.

The license test. Here is the point the whole pitch depends on you missing: the people teaching you to file tax forms are almost never licensed tax professionals. A real paid tax preparer works under rules — CPAs, enrolled agents, and attorneys answer to licensing boards, and anyone paid to prepare tax returns must hold an IRS Preparer Tax Identification Number (PTIN) and sign the returns they prepare. The guru on a livestream holds none of it, signs nothing, and carries none of the risk. There is a reason filing instruction is licensed work — when the form is wrong, or worse, fraudulent, the liability does not land on the teacher. It lands on the name signed at the bottom of the filing: yours. The teacher is not always out of reach, either: the IRS and the Department of Justice pursue scheme promoters with injunction suits and promoter penalties under 26 U.S.C. § 6700, and several prosecuted schemes started with the person doing the teaching. So ask the only question that matters before you take filing lessons from anyone: what is your license, and where is your name on my filing? No license and no signature means no accountability — just a fee.

Three questions, three strikes

Ask for three things: their credentials, one case their process won, one documented payout it produced. Strike one — the people teaching these filing processes hold no tax credentials: no license, no PTIN, nothing. Strike two — they have not beaten any cases; there is no court win they can show for the process. Strike three — they have shown no monetary gain from their own process, no proof it ever paid them or anyone else; the only money that moves is the fee the student pays them. Watch how fast the subject changes.

What to do instead. If a "strategy" requires you to file a tax form reporting a transaction that never happened, stop — that filing is itself the fraud, and your signature is on it. If you have already filed something like this, do not file more: talk to a tax professional about correcting the record promptly, because the IRS's 30-day withdrawal window after a frivolous-position notice can matter.

Scam #4: Bogus filings and paper terrorism

How it works. This trap runs in the other direction: the filing itself is the weapon. Because filing offices are largely ministerial — a facially complete form gets accepted and indexed — bad actors file baseless UCC-1s and liens against judges, prosecutors, police officers, lenders, ex-spouses, and business rivals to cloud their credit, stall their transactions, and harass them. The National Association of Secretaries of State and the International Association of Commercial Administrators document the pattern in their report on fraudulent UCC filings: these filings are "often used as a retaliatory measure," and clearing one can cost a victim thousands of dollars and take months or even years. Some promoters also sell this as a service or a tactic — filing liens against officials to "tie them up" or force a settlement.

The tell. There is no underlying loan, no signed security agreement, no transaction — just a grievance dressed as a lien. Legitimate filings trace back to a real deal you can paper. Bogus ones trace back to an argument.

What to do instead. If you are the target: you are not stuck with it. You can place an information statement on the record disputing the filing (§9-518; in Pennsylvania, the correction statement filed with the Department of State). Many states have added faster remedies — Minnesota's statute (§ 545.05), for example, gives a person named in a fraudulent financing statement an expedited judicial review to have it declared ineffective. Report the filing to your Attorney General and consult an attorney if it is blocking a loan or a closing. If someone is pitching you to file one of these: understand that filing a false lien against a federal judge or law-enforcement officer is a federal crime carrying up to 10 years in prison (18 U.S.C. § 1521), most states criminalize fraudulent filings as well, and a fraudulent filer can be liable to the victim for the greater of $10,000 or actual damages under statutes like Texas Business & Commerce Code § 9.5185. The "tactic" is a felony with a paper trail — your own filing.

And taking a false filing into court is a setup — for the filer. Some promoters coach the next step: file the bogus UCC-1 first, then wave it in court — sue on it, answer a real case with it, attach it to court papers to "establish" the claim. Understand what actually happens in that courtroom. The judge looks for the underlying transaction and finds none, and now the false filing is Exhibit A against the person holding it. Courts can strike the filing, sanction the filer, hold repeat filers in contempt, and refer false-lien cases for prosecution — more than a few followers have walked into court carrying a guru's paperwork and walked out with a criminal case of their own. The promoter, who never signed a single page, is nowhere in the room. Taking a fake lien into court does not trap the court. It traps you.

Scam #5: The fake "UCC department" — phishing emails, calls, and texts

How it works. The letter scam grows a digital twin. Emails, calls, and texts arrive claiming to be from a "UCC department" or filing office: urgent action required on your UCC filings, click here to verify, call this number, confirm your business and banking details to "release" or "maintain" your record. Banks that serve business customers — Middlesex Savings Bank's scam alert is one published example — warn that these messages push recipients to malicious websites or harvest personal information. Others simply demand payment for a copy of a UCC statement, the same public document from Scam #1, now with a link attached. The scammer's raw material is free: your name, your business, and your filing details are all sitting in the public record.

The tell. Real filing offices do not operate by surprise email, text, or robocall. Ohio's Secretary of State states flatly that its office does not send text alerts to business customers, and official state email comes from official state domains. Urgency is the weapon: a real record does not expire because you failed to click a link today — UCC-1s lapse on a 5-year schedule you can check yourself.

What to do instead. Never click, call back, or pay from the message itself. Open a fresh browser tab, go to your Secretary of State's official site yourself (in Pennsylvania, pa.gov's Department of State pages), and check your filing status there. Report phishing attempts to the FTC and your state Attorney General — and if you entered banking details anywhere, contact your bank immediately.

What a real UCC-1 actually costs — and where it's really filed

Here is the number every scam on this page is hoping you never learn: how much does it cost to file a UCC-1? Only what your state charges — a filing fee, paid to the state, once. There is no federal UCC office and no federal fee. A standard financing statement is filed centrally, with the Secretary of State (or equivalent central filing office) of the state where the debtor is located — in Pennsylvania, that is the Department of State, which takes filings online and by paper. Fees are set by each state and vary more than most people expect:

Pennsylvania$84 flat — regardless of pages or number of debtors; amendments and correction statements are also $84; a UCC-11 information request is $12PA Department of State fee schedule; 19 Pa. Code § 3.30

So the honest range runs from single digits in some states to Pennsylvania's $84 flat fee — one of the highest in the country. What it never runs to is $300, $500, or $2,000 for the act of filing. When a "service" or a "package" costs ten times the state fee, you are not buying a filing. You are buying the seller's story about the filing. Red-flag checklist: 12 signs it's a UCC filing scam

  • It looks like a bill from the government but the sender is a company — "UCC Statement Service," "UCC Processing Center," or similar invented-agency names.
  • The price is many times the state fee — $90–$107 for a document the state sells for $1–$15, or hundreds for a filing that costs $5–$84.
  • The fine print confesses — "not affiliated with any government agency," "this is a solicitation." Believe it.
  • Urgency and deadlines for something you never ordered — real filing offices don't invoice you by mail for copies.
  • A filing is promised to discharge debts, erase taxes, or change your legal status. A UCC-1 does none of those things.
  • You're told to file a UCC-1 against yourself — or against a "strawman" — to unlock an account. There is no account.
  • You're told to file a 1099-A or 1099-OID to "get your money." That filing is the fraud, and your signature is on it.
  • Your "teacher" has no license and signs nothing. No CPA, enrolled agent, attorney, or PTIN — just a checkout page. The liability is yours alone.
  • No credentials, no won cases, no proof of gain — just a checkout page.
  • You're coached to file liens against judges, officials, or lenders to pressure them. That's paper terrorism, and it is prosecuted.
  • An email, text, or call from a "UCC department" wants you to click, call, or hand over banking details. Filing offices don't work that way.
  • No real loan and no signed security agreement — but they still want you to file. Without the deal, there is nothing legitimate to file about.

UCC-1 scam FAQ

How much does it cost to file a UCC-1?

The filing fee is set by your state. Pennsylvania charges a flat $84 to file a financing statement with the Department of State, regardless of pages or number of debtors. Other states are cheaper — Texas charges $5 for a standard UCC filing and North Carolina charges $38 for a one-to-two-page statement. Anyone charging you hundreds of dollars just to submit the form is adding a markup the state never sees.

I got a letter from a "UCC Statement Service." Is it real?

It is a private solicitation, not a government notice. Companies using names like "UCC Statement Service" pull your information from public filing records and mail official-looking letters offering copies of your UCC statement for $90 to $107 — documents your Secretary of State sells for a few dollars, and in some states lets you download free. Secretaries of State and Attorneys General in states including Tennessee, Texas, Ohio, and North Carolina have issued public warnings about these mailings. Read the fine print: it usually admits the sender is not a government agency.

Can filing a UCC-1 on myself discharge my debts or unlock Treasury money?

No. A UCC-1 is only a public notice of a real security interest created by a signed security agreement. It moves no money, opens no account, and discharges no debt. The IRS lists the "straw man account" and redemption theories behind this pitch as frivolous (IRS Notice 2010-33), and each frivolous submission carries a $5,000 penalty under 26 U.S.C. § 6702. Promoters of these schemes have gone to federal prison.

What is IRS Form 1099-A actually for?

Form 1099-A, "Acquisition or Abandonment of Secured Property," is an information return a lender files when it acquires secured property (for example, through foreclosure) or learns the property has been abandoned. The borrower receives a copy for their tax records. It is not a form an individual files to claim money, and filing one to generate a refund is the core of a prosecuted fraud scheme.

Someone filed a bogus UCC-1 against me. What can I do?

You have options. You can file an information statement (§9-518; in Pennsylvania it is the correction statement) putting your dispute on the record. Many states also provide expedited court or administrative processes to have fraudulent financing statements declared ineffective — Minnesota's expedited judicial review statute (§ 545.05) is one example. Report the filing to your state Attorney General and the FTC, and consult an attorney if the filing is blocking a loan, sale, or closing.

The real version: master the UCC, stand in your private jurisdiction

Here is what the scam version is imitating. There is a real skill here, and it is worth having: to master the UCC means knowing what the Code actually says — what a security interest is, how perfection and priority work, which office takes which filing, what a form does and just as clearly what it does not do — and then using that knowledge on real transactions: real agreements, signed on paper, between real parties, filed at the real office for the real fee. Standing in your private jurisdiction looks like the same discipline from the other side: your own affairs kept in order, privately and lawfully — your agreements documented, your records kept, your filings accurate, your books your own. No guru can file you into that position, and no form can substitute for it. It is built out of knowledge, and knowledge is the one thing on this page the scammers are counting on you not to have.

The same test sorts the legitimate versions of the other phrases the gurus borrow. To master your estate means your own house in order — your assets titled correctly, your agreements in writing, your records kept — not a package that declares your estate untouchable. To master your status means knowing exactly who you are in each transaction — debtor, secured party, trustee, beneficiary — and what that role can and cannot do under the actual law, instead of paying someone to "correct" a status that was never broken. Acting Sui Juris — in your own right, as a competent adult managing your own affairs — is a posture of knowledge and responsibility, not a filing you buy. And a private express trust is a real instrument: a trust created deliberately, by a written declaration, with a named trustee and real property actually transferred into it, doing exactly what trust law says it does — no more, and no less. Every one of these is legitimate. Every one of them is also slower, plainer, and more paper-heavy than the pitch — which is precisely how you can tell them apart from the pitch.
