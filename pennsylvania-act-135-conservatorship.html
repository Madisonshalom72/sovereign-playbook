Pennsylvania Act 135 Conservatorship: How to File to Take Control of a Blighted, Abandoned Property

By Madison Hoskins • UCC Talk24 • Published November 16, 2026 • Educational guide — not legal advice

A house on your street has sat empty for five years — roof failing, porch pulling away, owner gone or unreachable, and the whole block paying for it. Pennsylvania law has an answer that is neither a fine nor a demolition order: an Act 135 conservatorship. Under the Pennsylvania conservatorship act for abandoned property — formally the Abandoned and Blighted Property Conservatorship Act, the Conservatorship Act of 2008 — a court can appoint a conservator to take control of a blighted, abandoned property, rehabilitate it, and, if the owner never steps up, see it sold into new hands. This guide teaches the whole route: who may file, what the property must look like on paper, the filing steps in the Court of Common Pleas, what a conservator may and may not do, and how the owner can still reclaim the property.

What Act 135 conservatorship is

The Abandoned and Blighted Property Conservatorship Act — Act 135 of 2008, codified at 68 P.S. §§ 1101–1120 — was signed on November 26, 2008 and took effect on February 24, 2009; amendments in 2014 strengthened it. It answers a problem the ordinary tools handle badly: a building whose owner is absent, unresponsive, or unable to act, rotting in place while fines pile up unpaid and the neighbors absorb the damage.

The Act's answer is a conservator: a person or organization the court appoints to take possession and control of the building and its land for one purpose — rehabilitation. The proceeding is in rem — it runs against the property itself — and it is filed in the Court of Common Pleas of the county where the building sits. Understand the shape of it before anything else: a blighted property conservatorship is control to fix, under a court's supervision, on a court-supervised path. It is not a purchase, not a taking, and not a shortcut to ownership — the sections below keep coming back to that line, because it is the line that separates this statute from every pitch built around it.

On the record

The first great test of the Act, the Germantown Conservancy litigation (995 A.2d 451, Pa. Cmwlth. 2010), confirmed the Act's machinery — petition, conditions, hearing — while insisting that each property be proved up on its own facts. Blight is not a slogan in this statute; it is a checklist, and the checklist is the lesson.

Who may petition: the party in interest

Not just anyone can start an Act 135 case. Only a "party in interest" may petition, and the Act defines the class: the owner; a lienholder; a resident or business owner located within 2,000 feet of the building; a nonprofit corporation that has a project within five miles of the building; the municipality; or the school district.

Read that list again, because it is the Act's boldest feature: a neighbor living with the blight has standing to start the case. You do not need to own the building, hold its mortgage, or work for the city. If you live or run a business within 2,000 feet of a qualifying property, the statute hands you the petition. The same is true across the Commonwealth — the definition is statewide, not a local rule — so the neighbor in Wilkes-Barre, the nonprofit in Pittsburgh, and the lienholder in Erie all work from the same list.

Does the property qualify? Four conditions, three blight factors

Standing gets you to the courthouse door. The property itself must then qualify, and the proof is specific. Four conditions must ALL hold:

  1. Unoccupied. The building has not been legally occupied for at least twelve months.
  2. Not being sold in good faith. The owner has not shown active marketing of the building in the preceding sixty days at a good-faith price.
  3. Not in a private foreclosure. The building is not already in a foreclosure action brought by a private party.
  4. Not freshly bought. The owner has not shown that he acquired the building within the preceding six months.

On top of those four, the court must find at least three blight factors — among them:

  • The building is a public nuisance.
  • The building needs substantial rehabilitation.
  • The building is unfit for habitation or use.
  • The building poses a health or safety hazard.
  • The building harms the community's economic well-being.

This is where Act 135 cases are won and lost: not on outrage about the property, but on documentation. Code citations, inspection records, photographs, and a timeline of vacancy are the evidence that carries the checklist — assemble them before you file, not after.

How to file for conservatorship of a building in Pennsylvania

Here is the filing route, step by step, the way the Act builds it:

  1. Confirm your standing. Place yourself on the party-in-interest list — owner, lienholder, resident or business owner within 2,000 feet, nonprofit with a project within five miles, municipality, or school district. If you are not on the list, you cannot petition.
  2. Prove the property up on paper. Document the four conditions (twelve months unoccupied, no good-faith marketing in sixty days, no private foreclosure pending, no acquisition in the last six months) and at least three blight factors, with code citations and records attached.
  3. Name a conservator and a plan. The petition carries a recommended conservator and a preliminary rehabilitation plan with cost estimates and funding sources. A petition without a credible plan and credible money behind it is a petition asking the court to hand a building to a wish.
  4. File the petition in the Court of Common Pleas of the county where the building sits, with the required sworn statement. The case is in rem — it proceeds against the property.
  5. Go to the hearing. The Act sets the hearing within 120 days of filing, and all interested parties may be heard — the owner included. Expect the court to test every condition and every blight factor against your proof.
  6. If the court appoints the conservator, the conservator takes possession and control and the rehabilitation begins under court supervision — the phase the next section covers.

Filing fees, local rules, and forms vary by county — check the Court of Common Pleas and the county's own published procedures where the building sits before you file. What does not vary is the statute's checklist above.

What the conservator may — and may not — do

What the conservator may do. Once appointed, the conservator takes possession and control of the building and its land. It may borrow and spend its own funds to rehabilitate the property, and it holds a lien on the property for its costs — the security that makes the fronted money recoverable. The work runs under the court's supervision, step by step.

What the conservator may not do — the limits written into the design:

  • It does not become the owner. Possession and control are not title. The deed stays in the owner's name unless and until a court orders a sale at the end of the process.
  • It cannot sell the property on its own timetable. A sale requires a court order, and the court may order one only after the conservator has held control for at least six months.
  • It cannot treat the building as its own asset. The appointment exists for rehabilitation under the plan the court approved; the conservator's recovery is its lawfully spent costs and its statutory fee — not the property.
  • It cannot erase the owner. The owner's right to reclaim the property runs through the whole case, as the next section explains.

The owner's way back: reclaiming the property

The owner is not simply erased by an Act 135 petition. At the hearing, the owner may be heard like every other interested party. And after appointment, the owner holds the Act's built-in exit: he may reclaim the property by paying what the conservator has lawfully spent. The conservator's lien secures those documented costs — the money actually and lawfully put into the building — and paying them ends the conservatorship with the property back in the owner's hands, rehabilitated at the owner's expense rather than the neighborhood's.

That design is the statute's fairness, and its discipline: the conservator is protected for real money really spent, and the owner is protected against losing a building over anything less.

How it ends: the court-ordered sale

If the owner does not reclaim the property, the case has a second ending. Once the conservator has held control for at least six months, the court may order the property sold — the blight ends in new ownership. From the outcome, the conservator's lien is paid, along with its fee, which the 2014 amendments set at the greater of $2,500 or twenty percent of rehabilitation costs or sale proceeds, per practitioner accounts of the amended Act.

So the full arc, stated plainly: petition → proof → hearing → appointment → rehabilitation under supervision → owner redeems, or the court sells. Every step is on the record, in a courtroom, with the checklist doing the work.

Costs and timeline: the honest part

Read this before you file anything

Act 135 is a court process, not a shortcut. The conservator fronts real money — its own funds, borrowed and spent on a building it does not own — and recovers only through its lien, on the court's schedule. The hearing alone can take up to 120 days from filing; a sale cannot come before six months of control; the honest timeline is measured in seasons, not weeks. And the sentence that matters most: conservatorship is control to fix — the property is NOT simply "yours." Anyone who teaches Act 135 as a trick for taking houses cheap is teaching you a case you will lose. It is a remedy for people prepared to do the work: real standing, real proof, real money, real rehabilitation.

The Luzerne County picture: conservatorship beside the other lawful doors

Act 135 does not stand alone — it is one of several lawful routes Pennsylvania built for problem property, and knowing which door fits which property is the real skill. In Luzerne County, the ground truth is familiar: blighted and abandoned parcels scattered through Wilkes-Barre, Hazleton, Pittston, and the boroughs, many of them cycling through the county Tax Claim Bureau's lists.

  • The tax claim ladder (Real Estate Tax Sale Law, Act 542 of 1947): when taxes go unpaid, the county sells — upset sale first (liens survive), then judicial sale (free and clear by court order), then the repository list of unsold parcels open to written bids, with the county, municipality, and school district all approving. Slow, documented, and no warranty — research the parcel before money moves.
  • Land banks (Land Bank Act, Act 153 of 2012): public authorities that gather vacant and tax-delinquent parcels, clear title, and return them to productive use — a counterparty for buyers whose plans answer the land bank's mission.
  • Act 135 conservatorship: the door that fits the building the tax ladder cannot fix — the occupied-block eyesore with a living but absent owner, where what the property needs is not a buyer at auction but a conservator with a plan, a budget, and a judge.

Different doors, different titles, different timelines — all of them running on the same three rails: a statute, a public record, and time. That discipline is taught end to end in our free course's land module — see the offers below.

Act 135 conservatorship FAQ

What is Act 135 conservatorship in Pennsylvania?

A court proceeding under the Abandoned and Blighted Property Conservatorship Act (Act 135 of 2008, 68 P.S. §§ 1101–1120). A party in interest petitions the Court of Common Pleas; if the building is proved abandoned and blighted under the statute's checklist, the court appoints a conservator who takes possession and control to rehabilitate it. The conservator does not become the owner.

Who can file for conservatorship of a building in Pennsylvania?

Only a party in interest: the owner; a lienholder; a resident or business owner within 2,000 feet of the building; a nonprofit corporation with a project within five miles; the municipality; or the school district.

What must be proved for a property to qualify?

All four conditions — unoccupied for at least twelve months, no good-faith marketing shown in the preceding sixty days, no private-party foreclosure pending, and no owner acquisition shown within the preceding six months — plus at least three blight factors, such as public nuisance, substantial rehabilitation needed, unfitness for habitation or use, health or safety hazard, or harm to the community's economic well-being.

Does the conservator own the property?

No. The conservator holds possession and control for rehabilitation, and a lien for its costs. Title stays with the owner unless the court orders a sale — which it may do only after the conservator has held control for at least six months.

Can the owner get the property back?

Yes — the owner may reclaim the property by paying what the conservator has lawfully spent. The redemption right is built into the Act.

How is Act 135 different from buying on the repository list?

A repository purchase buys a tax-delinquent parcel from the county's unsold list, with all three taxing districts approving the bid and no warranty of title. An Act 135 conservatorship takes control of a blighted building — including one whose owner is present on paper but absent in fact — to force its rehabilitation under court supervision. Different problem, different door: the repository moves parcels nobody claimed at sale; conservatorship fixes buildings nobody is fixing at all.
