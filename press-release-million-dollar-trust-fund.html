How to build a million dollar trust fund is one of the most-asked wealth questions online — and one of the least answered. The results that rank for it are written for couples who already have millions in excess wealth, while the comment sections underneath fill up with ordinary people asking where a trust is established, who qualifies, and what order the pieces go in. A new book written for those commenters, “Generate a $1,000,000 in 30 Days or Less” by Madison Hoskins, takes the question head-on — and says the order most people assume is backwards.

Prepare, protect, maintain — in that order

The book is not written to a reader chasing a million. It is written to a reader who has one — on paper, the day his position is structured — and whose real job is the three disciplines in its tagline: Start. Manage. Grow. Its governing line is printed early and repeated often: “Money is attracted to good management and runs away from bad management.” The structure the book teaches at headline level: the trust is created before the policy, the policy becomes the reader’s own bank, and the capital is deployed into undervalued property — with the management systems to keep what gets built. Inside, readers get the steps — the steps to generate the position, and the steps to maintain it: the management systems for holding million-dollar and multi-million-dollar estates once they are built. This book will give you the education that you need to succeed. The steps, the figures, and the plays themselves are the book: 332 pages, 40 chapters and two appendices, including an AI prompt pack and a verified resource list of 16 life insurance carriers.

The questions readers keep asking in public

The research behind this release started where the questions live: in the comment sections under the videos and posts carrying these exact phrases. The same questions come back, worded a dozen different ways. Where is a trust like this established — and who can establish one? Does the policy come before the trust, or after? Is the money in a million dollar policy spendable the day it binds, or does it build? Can a note a man holds stand behind a loan the way a house does? How long before a whole life policy can be borrowed against — and what was decided before the first premium was ever paid that sets that answer? None of those questions gets a straight public answer today. They get a sales pitch, an invitation to a private message thread, or silence. This book was written to be the public answer — in print, in order, with the steps inside the book instead of behind a sales call.

The second question nobody answers

The release’s research also surfaced a second unanswered question: can you use a promissory note as collateral for a loan? Search results answer the opposite question — how a lender takes collateral for a note — while the holder’s side of the table, pledging a note you hold, goes untaught in public. The book gives that question its own chapter, alongside the alternate method it is known for among early readers. What the method is, and how the numbers run, is inside the book.

Prepare the structure, protect what it holds, then maintain it like a man who intends to keep it — that’s the whole game, and it’s why the book is priced where a working man can reach it.”— Madison Hoskins, author

Frequently asked questions

Q: How do you build a million dollar trust fund?
A: That is the book’s title question, and its answer starts with sequence: what gets created first, what it holds, and how it is managed afterward. The full structure — chapters, figures, and plays — is the book itself, available at the link above.
Q: Can you use a promissory note as collateral for a loan?
A: The book devotes a chapter to the holder’s side of that question — the side public results do not teach. The chapter is in the book; the question is why readers keep asking it in comment sections with no public answer.
Q: Should you create a trust before buying a life insurance policy?
A: The book’s position is in its framing: preparation comes before protection, and protection before growth. Which order, and why it matters, is laid out in the trust chapters.
Q: What is a million dollar life insurance policy, and is the money spendable on day one?
A: The book answers that question honestly in its opening chapters — what is real the day the policy binds, and what builds over time — because the honest version of that answer is the foundation everything else stands on.
Q: Can a life insurance policy be used as collateral?
A: The book treats the policy as an asset that can stand behind a loan — and separates that move from borrowing against the policy itself, a distinction the public answers blur into one. Which route fits which situation, and what each one requires, is worked out in the collateral chapters.
Q: How do you use a life insurance policy to buy real estate?
A: In the book’s frame, the policy is not the finish line — it is the funding position behind the purchase. How a purchase is structured on that position, and in what order the pieces move, is the work of the deployment chapters.
Q: What is the gap method in flipping paper?
A: The gap method is the book’s named play for flipping paper, and it is taught whole — in its own chapters, not in summaries. What the gap is, where it comes from, and how a position in it is serviced is the teaching, and the teaching is in the book.
Q: How do you write a million dollar promissory note?
A: The book’s alternate method starts with paper the reader writes himself. What that paper has to carry to be taken seriously, and what happens to it after it is written, is the chapter. The question is the headline; the instrument is in the book.
Q: What is the waterfall method for trusts?
A: The waterfall method is the book’s name for the way its trusts are layered, and the order in which things move between them once they are built. The structure, drawn out in full, is in the trust chapters — it is a chart that has to be seen, not a slogan that can be repeated.
Q: How do you stack life insurance policies?
A: More than one policy, layered on purpose — personal, family, and business policies arranged as one position instead of scattered purchases. Which layers, in what order, and what each layer is for is drawn out in the book’s policy chapters.
Q: How do you get your money off the table in real estate?
A: That is the book’s name for the moment a deal pays its own money back out. Which money comes off the table first, and in what order, is the play — and the play, with its figures, is in the book.
Q: How long before you can borrow against a whole life policy?
A: The honest answer depends on decisions made when the policy is designed — which is why the book gives the timing question its own roadmap near the end. What is real on day one, what builds on a schedule, and what sets the schedule is laid out there, in order.
Q: What is a modified endowment contract, and why does it matter?
A: It is the design line every policy buyer should know exists before he signs — a line drawn through the policy’s design that changes how the policy is treated from that point forward. Where the line sits, what crosses it, and what crossing it costs is taught in the policy design chapters.
Q: What ai prompts find undervalued real estate?
A: The book closes with an appendix of the prompts the author uses to scout undervalued property — the scout work that comes before an offer is ever made. The prompts themselves are the appendix; they are printed in the book and nowhere else.
Q: Is this infinite banking?
A: The book works in the same vehicle family but is not an infinite banking book: it adds the trust-first sequence, note collateral, and property deployment plays. The comparison is answered in full on the book’s page.
